## What the captioned lessons actually teach

**1.2 Clarity (18 min).** Name your why (hours, balance, salary, culture).
List what you would sacrifice (location, commute, a dip in pay for the right
door) and your non-negotiables (benefits, leave, retirement). Do the hourly
math: a $50k salary at 55 hours a week is $17.50 an hour, so a $48k job at 40
hours pays more. Her survey: 28% of her students took a pay cut, 98% of those
do not regret it; newer teachers usually earn more immediately. Ends with a
commitment sentence to write in a planner. Personal story: she left at rock
bottom, depression and stress illness, and the next job "seemed like a scam"
because it was too good.

**3.1 Résumé (20 min).** Three mistakes: not enough credit, teacher lingo,
no posting keywords. Job duty vs accomplishment ("teaches sixth grade" vs
"built a 12-week programme for 29 participants in a month"). Keep a career
accomplishments journal and pull from it per application. Honest title
adjustments only (learning consultant to corporate trainer, never to
director). "Educator transitioning to X" as the headline. Translate students
to participants or clients, parents to stakeholders, grade level to
department. The one genuinely clever move: if a posting wants CSAT or KPI
experience, send families a one-question satisfaction survey now, track it,
and you own a truthful metric before you leave. Ends with the emotional
reality (you will cry over the draft) and "it only takes one yes".

**3.2 ATS (26 min).** Keywords plus acronyms, core competencies two or three
times, chronological format only, one and a half to two pages, PDF unless
told otherwise, no colour, charts, Canva or templates. Section order: header,
summary in the target title, experience, community leadership, education
(no dates), technology. Keep three role-specific base résumés. Red-flag
wording: "position eliminated", never "fired" or "left for growth". Age
bias: list 10 to 15 years only. Gaps: older than five years and under two,
ignore; inside five years and over eight months, fill with volunteering. Apply
at a 70% match. A referral bypasses the scanner. A long warning about
Googling résumé advice: outdated, selling templates, or just opinion.

**Remote bonus (18 min).** Flexibility means different things; client-facing
remote roles expect you at a desk. Her survey of about 400 former teachers:
90% could not watch their children full time while working remotely. Most
remote jobs are location-restricted, so search USA, then state, then city.
Skip LinkedIn Easy Apply, go to the careers page, apply to one role per
company. Hiring managers fear two things: people gaming remote work and
people who cannot self-manage. Show tech self-sufficiency and self-starter
stories; a warm referral beats everything. LinkedIn venting is a red flag.

**5.1 text block.** Three HR "code phrases" for why you are leaving (direction
change, management change, no room to grow), each with the situation it
covers and a warning that "no room to grow" reads as flaky under three years.
Portfolio advice: build a fake customer-learning portal for a company that
does not exist. Six questions to ask the interviewer, including "any reason
you would not hire me?".

**FAQ highlights.** Nobody searches LinkedIn for "former teacher", they search
"customer success", so rebrand to the target title. Apply two months before
you can start. One or two applications a week means a year of searching;
fifteen to twenty is aggressive. No reply is normal; follow up weekly.

## Where she is stronger than us

1. **Proof from her own alumni.** Salary and title data, interview questions
   "former teachers were asked", survey percentages, spotlights. Every
   number comes from her students, which is unfalsifiable and personal. Our
   numbers come from Glassdoor and the BLS.
2. **Static example résumés by target role.** Eight PDFs a cold reader can
   open before paying.
3. **Community loop.** Circle group, badge on landing, alumni group, spotlight
   form. Stories feed the marketing and the marketing feeds the stories.
4. **Volume of encouragement.** "Hang in there" lessons close two modules;
   1.2 and 3.1 both end on the emotional cost. She names the crying.
5. **Concrete hacks.** The CSAT survey, the hourly-rate math, the three code
   phrases, the fake portal portfolio.

## Where we are stronger

1. Interactive lessons that carry answers forward (verdict, dials, energy
   audit, path map). Hers is watch-and-write-in-a-PDF.
2. Sourced numbers with the source under each lesson. Several of hers are
   unsourced ("75% of résumés never get seen", "300 to 600 applicants").
3. The stage map. She has one "clarity" lesson; we have five stages, a free
   verdict, and offers matched to stage.
4. Captions and text. Most of her videos have neither, so the course is
   unsearchable and inaccessible. Ours is read-and-do.
5. Guardrails. Her upskilling page is an affiliate shop; ours says when a
   certificate does not help and why Epic cannot be bought.
6. Currency. Much of hers is 2022.

# Part 2: the workbook, the downloads, and what to change in Transition OS (2026-09-18, later the same day)

Read after James's go-ahead: the 121-page editable workbook, the 24-page
"Former Teacher Salaries and Job Titles" survey, and all eight example
résumés. Video transcripts for the 25 uncaptioned lessons are being produced
locally with faster-whisper; findings from those get appended when they land.
Everything below is still a summary in my words.

## What the workbook is

The workbook is the course. Each video gets a page with "three key lessons"
(so the video content is recoverable from it), a personal note, and then two
to eight worksheet pages. It is 2022 Canva design, a lot of it, and about a
quarter is generic career-coaching filler (a career wheel, a mind map, a work
style page, three pages of affirmations, a book list). The other three
quarters is specific and good. By module:

**Module 1.** A "keep it private" rule sheet (no searching on school
computers, work email off the résumé, LinkedIn broadcast off, don't list the
current employer as a reference, what to do if they find out). Clarity pages:
your why, how fast you need out, what you would sacrifice (six yes/no
questions: relocate, pay dip, no benefits, must be remote, need people
around, would commute), required schedule, and a commitment sentence. A
salary-needs calculator (monthly expenses, add 20% for tax, times twelve). A
real-hourly-rate page (39 weeks x 60 hours = 2,340 hours; $55k is $23 an hour;
the same $55k at 40 hours is $26 and 260 hours back). Retirement rollover
basics. A time-management lesson with the 60/30/10 split (build experience,
network, learn), Big Monthly Goals, a month-by-month calendar keyed to the
school year (apply hard six to eight weeks before you can start), a monthly
goal tracker (applications, contacts, companies researched, hours
upskilling), and a twelve-week 1-to-5 self-check on happiness, productivity,
health, self-care and confidence. Warm networking: who to add on LinkedIn
first (past coworkers, alumni, church and volunteer groups, teachers who
left, friends, relatives, people who sell you things), a LinkedIn setup
checklist, and the rule that "transitioning teacher" as an identity reads as
"will take anything". Mindset: her stat that 60% of her students felt like
giving up right before it worked, "practice shipping", a "negativity stopper"
person, top five strengths from colleagues, rewards for small, medium and big
milestones.

**Module 2.** Four career buckets (stay in education with children, without
children, at an education company, outside education), each with a job-title
list; then a department tour (sales, HR, R&D, marketing, finance) with "why
you'd be good at it". A classroom-to-corporate translation table (grade
books = documentation and compliance; parent communication = customer
service, de-escalation, stakeholder engagement; committees = project
management; lesson planning = instructional design and content development;
students = clients or participants; principals = senior leadership). Three
experience bands (0 to 5 years: higher-paying entry roles, 85% earn more; 6
to 10: best of both; 10+: manager-level L&D, ID, CS, senior curriculum). A
"new terminology" log to define later. A three-career worksheet (title
variations, salary, networked yet?, 60 to 70% match?, skills to add). A growth
ladder page (corporate trainer to training manager to VP) to answer "where
does this go". Company pages: companies you have an "in" with, companies
whose products you already use plus their competitors, top lists, large
companies near you. Cold networking: LinkedIn alumni search, mentor search
(friend of a friend first, longest tenure best), and five elevator pitches by
target role with the reasoning under each.

**Module 3.** The career accomplishments journal: seven pages of prompts
with blanks for numbers (students taught in N years, teachers trained,
resources created, test scores up X% in Y months, deadlines beaten, budgets
managed, grants secured, events for N people, parent correspondence cadence,
LMS used, committees, awards). Then two pages of teaching-experience to
corporate-translation pairs, a skills circle-list, self-rated scales, a unique
selling points page. ATS: a do/don't table, gaps template (freelance,
part-time, school volunteering, community volunteering), résumé checklist,
portfolio planning (thank-you emails, work samples, screenshots of wins).
Cold networking II: five to ten reach-outs a week in season, conferences,
groups of people already happy in the industry, not other job seekers.
"Professionalism and past trauma": people hire candidates who want in, not
out; stop saying "I'm just learning"; "this will be the greatest acting job
of your life".

**Module 4.** Applications checklist (one concise transition sentence in the
cover letter, skills matched, an insider nudge, follow-up at one week,
portfolio needed?), salary research per company, benefits checklist (401k
match, stock, stipends, PTO, remote, parental leave, bonus), culture pages to
read, a "busy but not productive" audit, and the "hang in there" page with
"what will you do when you feel like giving up" and "how will you treat
yourself for every rejection".

**Module 5.** Know yourself (five strongest attributes for this role,
salary you want, strength and weakness), know the job (bullets you have
direct experience with, acronyms to study), know the company (two-sentence
description, mission alignment), "when can you start" and contract timing,
follow-up timing rules (interview before 2 pm: email between 2 and 4 the same
day; after 2 pm: 9 am next morning; second follow-up only after the stated
timeline; then every two weeks), salary negotiation (research before the
offer, know your walk-away number, decide in advance whether you'd take the
first offer), a weighted pros-and-cons sheet (1 to 5 per item, sum each
side, then the eyes-closed gut check), and how to answer a no (reply, say
why you were excited, ask about future roles).

## What the salary survey shows

About 500 self-reported rows: job category, title, starting band, state,
band after a promotion. Bands are $20k wide (under $50k; $50 to 70k; $70 to
90k; $90 to 109k; over $109k). Categories: customer success and client
services, HR and talent, instructional design and L&D (the largest, roughly
half the rows), marketing and communications, operations and admin, project
and program management, sales.

The pattern, which is the useful part: most first roles start in the $50 to
70k band, a sizable minority in $70 to 90k, and the after-promotion column
moves one band up for most people who filled it in, often two. Sales and PM
show the biggest jumps ($50 to 70k in, $90 to 109k after). Ops and admin
show the smallest. The honest summary is "the first job is the door, the
second title is the raise", and that is a line worth having our own data
behind.

She uses the survey three ways: a downloadable PDF inside lesson 2.2, a live
spreadsheet inside 5.3 for negotiation, and quoted percentages in the videos
(28% took a pay cut, 98% of those don't regret it; 85% of under-five-year
leavers earn more; 60% felt like giving up right before it worked; 90% of
remote workers couldn't watch their kids full time). All of it is her own
students, so nobody can argue with it.

## What the eight résumés have in common

Same skeleton every time, which is the point:

- Name, city, phone, email, LinkedIn. One line.
- Target title as a headline ("Customer Success Manager"), then a row of six
  skill pills under it.
- A summary paragraph that starts "[Adjective] educator transitioning to
  [role] with N+ years of experience in [three corporate nouns]" and then
  lists what they've demonstrated, in the target role's vocabulary.
- Experience, reverse-chronological, "Company | City, ST" left, dates right,
  the honest job title in italics under it, then six to nine bullets. Every
  bullet opens with a verb, most carry a number, and the nouns are
  translated: clients, stakeholders, participants, KPIs, compliance,
  onboarding, senior leadership. A separate "Key accomplishments" sub-list
  when there are big ones ("reduced deficit by $750k in 4 months").
- Education without dates. Certifications. A technical proficiencies line
  that names the tools in the posting (Salesforce, Asana, SQL, Tableau).

Two are two pages (senior people), the rest one. No colour, no columns, no
graphics. The "low-level unrelated" example is the most instructive: a
five-year elementary teacher aimed at account manager, with a college
bookstore job from 2010 kept in because it's the only sales line.

## What to change in Transition OS, mapped to our modules

Ordered by leverage. Each item says where it goes and roughly what it is.
Nothing here copies her pages; these are the ideas, rebuilt for SLPs and in
our format (interactive, saved answers, sourced).

### 1. Build the proof engine (touches Modules 7 and the sales pages)

- **Alumni salary and title sheet.** Add four optional fields to the Module 7
  "Tell your story" action: path, title, starting band, band now. Same bands
  she uses. Also ask it in the day-90 customer email and on a public "Where
  did you land?" form on slptransitions.com (her spotlight form, ours). At
  ten rows, publish the aggregate on `/course/ground` and the OS page as
  "Where SLPs from this program landed". At fifty, it becomes the lesson 2.1
  data next to the Glassdoor bands.
- **"Questions SLPs were actually asked" bank.** One field at the Module 6
  checkpoint ("what did they ask you?") and in the day-6 follow-up. Publish
  inside 6.3 grouped by path. Hers is the most-visited resource page in the
  course by link count, and it costs nothing after the first ten buyers.
- **Our own percentages.** Two questions we can start asking now via the
  quiz follow-ups and buyer emails: did your first non-clinical role pay
  more or less than clinical, and do you regret it. Her "28% took a cut,
  98% don't regret it" is the single most reassuring line in her course and
  we have no equivalent.

### 2. Add the money math to Module 1 (lesson 1.5, "What you can't afford to lose")

- **Real hourly rate.** Hours per week including notes at home and IEP prep,
  times weeks worked, divided into salary. Then the same for a 40-hour role
  at the same or slightly lower salary. SLP-native defaults: a school SLP at
  $65k working 50 hours over 40 weeks is $32.50 an hour; a $62k remote UR
  role at 40 hours over 48 weeks is $32.30 and 400 hours back. This is one
  interactive card; the dials already save the floor.
- **Salary floor from expenses.** Monthly expenses, plus 20% for tax, times
  twelve, gives the floor. Lets the floor be a number the reader computed,
  not a guess.
- **Six sacrifice questions.** Relocate, pay dip, no benefits, must be
  remote, need people around, would commute. Yes/no, saved, shown back on
  the workbook page. Our dials cover pay and distance from clinical; these
  cover the rest.

### 3. Add a "keep it private" sheet to Module 1 (lesson 1.7, beside "Tell one person")

Stage 1 is private doubt and we have nothing for the mechanics of staying
private. One page: no job searching on the work laptop or work email, work
email off the résumé, LinkedIn "share profile changes" off, "open to
recruiters" set to recruiters only, don't list the current employer as a
reference yet, what to say if a manager asks. For school SLPs add contract
and letter-of-intent dates; for medical SLPs add PRN and notice periods.

### 4. Add a time and calendar lesson to Module 3 or 6

We have no lesson on how much time this takes or when to spend it. Hers is
1.3 and it is one of her strongest. Ours: the 60/30/10 split (build proof,
talk to people, learn) with a weekly hours box; a school-year calendar for
school SLPs (apply hard six to eight weeks before you can start, so
April-May for an August start) and a rolling version for medical SLPs; the
FAQ's honest volume line (one or two applications a week means a year,
fifteen to twenty is aggressive); and the "busy but not productive" audit.
Put the monthly goal tracker (applications, conversations, companies,
hours) in the workbook.

### 5. Build the clinical accomplishments journal (Module 4, "Numbers you already have")

Her seven-page journal is the raw material her résumé lesson runs on. Ours
is one interactive lesson that saves, with SLP-native prompts: caseload size
and setting, evaluations per year, IEPs or care plans written, percentage of
goals met, families trained on home programs, staff trained on AAC or
dysphagia protocols, CFs supervised, EMR and documentation systems used,
productivity target held, denials overturned, discharge planning volume,
programs or protocols built, committees, in-services delivered, grants,
awards. Every answer feeds the Suite intake so the résumé tool starts with
numbers instead of asking for them. **This is the single biggest product
improvement in the list, because it fixes the Suite's known weak spot (the
résumé arrives with no numbers) upstream.**

### 6. Ship the clinic-to-corporate translation table (Module 4.2, and a workbook page)

Our pairs exist in the transferable-skills article and in `build_home.py`.
Make them a lesson page and a workbook page, in her two-column form, SLP
side left: students or patients to clients and participants; caregivers and
parents to stakeholders; IEP meeting to stakeholder meeting; caseload to
book of business; productivity to utilization or KPI; evaluation report to
assessment and analysis; SOAP notes to documentation compliance; in-service
to training delivery; AAC device setup to implementation and onboarding;
supervising CFs to people management; discharge planning to care
coordination; insurance authorization to payer relations. Add the one rule
that makes it honest: translate the noun, never the title.

### 7. The CSAT hack, SLP version (Module 4.3)

If a posting wants CSAT, NPS or KPI experience: run a one-question
satisfaction survey with caregivers or families for four to eight weeks,
anonymous, no PHI, track the average, set a target. That is a truthful
metric before you leave. Same for "retention": re-referral or attendance
rates you already have. Two paragraphs and a worksheet line.

### 8. Fix the résumé output against the eight-example skeleton (the Suite)

Check the Suite's résumé output against the skeleton above: target title as
headline, six skill pills, the "clinician transitioning to X with N years
in [three nouns]" summary, honest titles, verbs and numbers in every bullet,
a "Key accomplishments" sub-list, education without dates, a tools line
that names the posting's tools. Where the Suite differs, decide on purpose.
Then produce three static examples (liaison, customer success, utilization
review) from one anonymised real SLP résumé as a lead magnet and as proof on
the Suite page, the way her eight are proof inside the course.

### 9. Five elevator pitches by path (Module 3.2, "Messages that get answered")

Hers are 60 to 80 words each with the reasoning under them. Ours: liaison,
customer success, utilization review, clinical informatics, clinical
educator. Each one names years, the two things you'd want to do all day,
the target title, and the ask. In James's voice, second person.

### 10. LinkedIn hygiene and warm-first networking (Module 3.1)

Add: every comment and like sits on your profile like a line on your résumé;
no venting about clinical work, no "what should I do" posts; the
who-to-add-first list; alumni search; "open to recruiters" visible to
recruiters only. Then the referral truth from her remote lesson: an internal
referral is how a résumé gets read when the scanner rejected it.

### 11. Interview and offer mechanics (Module 6)

- 6.2: three honest ways to say why you're leaving without saying burnout,
  with the warning that "no room to grow" reads as flaky under three years.
  We have a lesson; check it has these.
- 6.3: the crowdsourced question bank (item 1).
- 6.5: benefits checklist (pension or 403(b) vesting versus 401k match, PTO,
  stipends, parental leave, bonus), the walk-away number decided before the
  offer, the weighted pros-and-cons sheet.
- New half-lesson or workbook page: follow-up timing rules and the two
  scripts; how to answer a no.
- "When can you start": contract dates for school SLPs, notice for medical.

### 12. Pension and vesting (Module 5.5, "Student loans, pay cuts and health insurance")

Her retirement page is teacher-specific (CalSTRS). Ours needs the school SLP
version (state pension vesting, 403(b)) and the medical version (401k
match, rollover). Two paragraphs with the "ask your plan, not a course"
disclaimer.

### 13. A morale beat before application season, in James's voice

She closes two modules with "hang in there" pages. Ours shouldn't
cheerlead, but Module 6 needs one honest page on what the wait is like: 42%
of postings close inside two weeks, silence is normal, weekly follow-ups,
keep applying until something is signed, and "what will you do when you
feel like giving up" as a saved answer that the checkpoint shows back.

### 14. Small additions

- Three experience bands (0 to 3, 4 to 10, 10+) on the path map with what
  each is realistically eligible for.
- "Where this path goes" next-title line on each path card.
- Title-variations field on the compare-paths tool (corporate trainer = L&D
  specialist = learning consultant).
- A licence line on the dashboard and in the workbook.
- The "negativity stopper" question folded into "Tell one person".
- Weekly 1-to-5 self-check row added to the week 1/6/12 check-in page.

## Not worth taking

The affirmation pages, the career wheel, the mind map, the work-style page,
