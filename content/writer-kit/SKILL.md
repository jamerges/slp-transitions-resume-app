# SLP Transitions Writer

Use this skill for writing, developing, or editing content for SLPTransitions.com and for closely related career-transition writing explicitly intended to sound like James Berges.

Do not use it for Osmind or other institutional healthcare writing.

## Load references deliberately

For any substantial draft or rewrite, read:

- `references/voice.md` for James's voice
- `references/style.md` for SLP Transitions editorial standards
- `references/audience.md` for the reader

Also read:

- `references/anti-slop.md` before final line editing
- `references/examples.md` when calibrating tone, structure, or example selection

Do not treat every reference example as equally authoritative. Follow the weighting notes in `examples.md`.

## Core objective

Produce writing that is:

1. intellectually curious
2. specific to SLPs
3. psychologically insightful without pretending to be therapy
4. research-informed without pop-science certainty
5. useful enough to change what the reader can do next
6. recognizably James rather than generic career content

The reader should feel accompanied, not coached at.

## Workflow

Adapt the workflow to the user's actual request. Do not force every step when the user already supplied what is needed.

### 1. Find the tension

Before writing, identify the real question or contradiction.

Useful forms:

- "I used to think X, but..."
- "SLPs are told X, yet..."
- "The obvious advice is X, but the harder problem is..."
- "Why does this feel so difficult even when the practical steps are clear?"

Do not begin with a definition merely because the article targets a keyword.

### 2. Establish the thesis

Privately complete:

> After reading this, I want an SLP to believe ______.

Pressure-test it:

- Is it specific?
- Is it defensible?
- Is it actually interesting?
- Could an intelligent SLP disagree?
- Does the piece have enough evidence or experience to earn it?

Generic encouragement is not a thesis.

### 3. Develop from real material

Prefer, in order:

- the user's own observation or experience
- an SLP-specific scenario
- interview material
- credible research
- a useful mental model
- a concrete analogy

If the user is trying to improve as a writer and has not asked for a finished draft, encourage a rough human-written or voice-dictated draft before polishing.

If the user explicitly asks for a finished draft, write it.

### 4. Structure around understanding

Organize the article according to how the reader's understanding should change, not according to SEO headings alone.

A common progression is:

observation or scene
→ tension
→ explanation or research
→ what it means for SLPs
→ complication or tradeoff
→ practical experiment
→ earned ending

Do not force this sequence if another structure is stronger.

### 5. Research honestly

Distinguish:

- evidence
- interpretation
- James's opinion
- personal experience
- interviewee experience

Do not turn one study into a universal psychological law.

Do not turn one career story into evidence that everyone should follow the same path.

For changing claims such as salary, hiring demand, licensure, certification, or AI impact, use current sources when tools are available.

### 6. Edit in layers

First edit the argument:

- What is the strongest idea?
- Is it buried?
- What is unsupported?
- What can be cut?
- Where would a smart skeptic object?

Then edit for the reader:

- Where does this become generic career advice?
- Does an SLP feel specifically understood?
- What question remains unanswered?
- Is there a practical next experiment?

Then edit the prose.

### 7. Run the anti-slop pass

Read `references/anti-slop.md`.

Treat flags as questions, not automatic rewrite commands.

Preserve intentional oddness, humor, fragments, contrasts, and rhetorical questions when they sound like James and do real work.

### 8. End without a manufactured moral

Prefer one of:

- return to the opening with new understanding
- give the reader one concrete experiment
- land on the most interesting implication

Do not automatically summarize the whole article.

## Learning loop

When meaningful feedback arrives, extract at most 1–3 durable lessons.

A durable lesson describes a repeatable pattern, not a one-off word choice.

Good:
- "James tends to bury the interesting claim behind too much setup."

Weak:
- "Use 'support' instead of 'help' in this sentence."

If the environment provides persistent project files, store durable lessons there rather than bloating this skill with temporary preferences.
