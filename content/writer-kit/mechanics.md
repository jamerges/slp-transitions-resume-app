# Sentence mechanics

James's standing style guide (2026-09-17), based on Strunk & White's concision
and Steven Pinker's *The Sense of Style*. It applies to every word this project
produces: articles, emails, product copy, lesson bodies, workbook pages, social
posts, Notion notes and replies in chat. The voice files say how James sounds;
this file says how a sentence is built. Where the two meet, both hold.

## 1. Start with the answer

No pre-framing, no throat-clearing, no "here's what you need to know". The
first sentence carries the point. A reader who stops there should still have
it.

## 2. No shell-noun-plus-colon labels

"Bottom line:", "Key takeaway:", "The reality:", "Pro tip:" are AI patterns.
Say the thing. A bold label at the head of a list item ("**Publish Jeannie
first.** Her interview is written.") is house format and stays; a label that
announces a sentence is coming is not.

## 3. Dashes

No em dashes. A comma, colon, or full stop does the job. If a dash is truly
unavoidable, use an en dash (–) with spaces. Number ranges keep a closed en
dash ($84k–$135k).

## 4. Omit needless words

Cut filler, meta-announcements ("Here is a breakdown of"), sycophantic openers
("Great question"), bureaucratic bloat ("in order to", "at this point in
time"), redundancies ("free gift", "end result") and hollow transitions
("That said", "Moving on", "It's worth noting").

## 5. Classic style: show the thing itself

Point at the fact, the mechanism, the person. Do not narrate the act of
explaining. "In this section we look at" and "It is important to note that"
are cut on sight. The reader is standing beside you looking at the same
object; describe the object.

## 6. Prune defensive hedging

"Arguably", "virtually", "somewhat", "it could be said", "in some ways",
"to a certain extent" are padding. State the claim. Keep a qualifier only when
the edge case is real and the reader needs it to act correctly, and then name
the edge case.

## 7. Curse of knowledge

Assume the reader is smart and has not seen the thing yet. Every abstraction
gets a concrete noun, a tangible mechanism, or a physical image. "The résumé
still says caseload where a recruiter needs to read clients" beats "the
résumé is not translated". Internal names (quest log, stage map, Ground) are
explained the first time or replaced with what they are.

## 8. Syntax

- **Kill nominalizations.** A concrete actor is the subject, and it does a
  strong verb. "She balked at the cost", not "her hesitation was cost-related".
  "Decide", not "reach a decision". "Apply", not "submit an application".
- **Resolve every pronoun** to a named antecedent, never to a whole prior
  clause. "She fixed the résumé", not "she fixed it" after two sentences of
  context.
- **Subject and verb close together, early.** Heavy clauses go at the end,
  where working memory can afford them.
- **Positive form.** "Assisted", not "not unassisted". Say what is, unless the
  negation is the point.
- **Emphatic information last.** The word the sentence exists for sits at the
  end, where the stress falls.

## 9. Edit before sending

Draft, then run the rules above as a pass, then send only the edited version.
The reader never sees the editing. Concretely: read the piece once for
pre-framing and labels, once for dashes and hedges, once for subject-verb
distance and pronouns, and once aloud for rhythm.
