The average corporate job opening now gets around 242 applications. That's up 111% since 2022.

And the recruiter reviewing that pile? They spend about 7.4 seconds on your resume before deciding whether you're a "maybe" or a "no."

Seven point four seconds. That's less time than it takes to say "individualized education program with measurable annual goals."

Which is exactly the problem.

Your clinical resume, the one full of caseloads, IEPs, dysphagia protocols, and SOAP notes, is written in a language the person doing the 7.4-second scan has never learned. They read it, fail to map it to their job, and move on to the next one. The rejection is about the document.

This is fixable, and you can fix it yourself. One SLP I learned about burned through a $220 resume writer, a $480 career coach, and a teacher-transition program before finding something that worked. What worked was translation. Let me show you how.

## Why does your clinical resume keep getting ignored?

Because nobody outside our field understands what SLPs actually do.

This comes up constantly in SLP career-change communities: "Nobody really understands what SLPs do or the transferable skills." Even generic career coaches admit they don't get it. You hand a recruiter a resume that says "managed a caseload of 55 students across 3 schools" and they see... a therapist. A very tired therapist. They miss the project manager, the client success professional, and the training specialist, even though you've been doing all three jobs at once, before lunch.

There were days when I felt like a customer service representative with three degrees. But my old resume didn't say "customer service" anywhere on it. It said "articulation," "fluency," and "AAC." A hiring manager can't map that. And in 7.4 seconds, they won't try.

The hidden rejection reasons for career changers go deeper than jargon, though. Hiring-side research keeps surfacing the same four:

1. **Skills described in clinical language the interviewer can't map** (the jargon problem)
2. **Retention doubt**, "will they bail and go back to clinical work?"
3. **No visible proof of investment in the new field**
4. **Salary mismatch fears**

Your resume can't fix all four alone, but it can fix the first one completely and chip away at the other three.

## Is the ATS really auto-rejecting your resume?

Probably not. And I want to spend a minute here because ATS panic sells a lot of bad resume advice.

You've seen the claim: "75% of resumes are auto-rejected by ATS before a human ever sees them." That number traces back to a 2012 sales pitch. In reality, roughly 92% of recruiters do *not* auto-reject candidates based on a match score. The ATS ranks. Humans reject.

That distinction matters more than it sounds. If a robot were rejecting you, the game would be keyword-stuffing. A *human* is rejecting you, after the software sorts the pile, so the game is being understandable and relevant at a glance.

Two more facts about the software:

**Modern systems match semantically.** Newer ATS platforms understand that "stakeholder communication" and "cross-functional collaboration" are related concepts. You don't need to paste the job description in white text at the bottom of the page. (Please don't do that. It's the resume equivalent of hiding vegetables in a smoothie and telling everyone about it.)

**The real filters are knockout questions.** The application form asking "Do you have 2+ years of SaaS experience?" is what screens people out automatically. The resume-reading robot with a grudge does not exist.

So where does formatting actually matter? Parsing. If the software can't read your resume, you can't get ranked. The rules are boring and simple:

- **Single column.** Those pretty two-column Canva templates can scramble in parsing.
- **Standard headers.** "Experience," "Skills," "Education", not "My Journey."
- **No tables, no graphics, no text boxes.** Save the visual creativity for your therapy materials.

That is the entire ATS strategy. Everything else is about the human.

## What resume format should a career-changing SLP use?

The combination format: a professional summary and a skills section at the top, work history below.

A chronological resume leads with your job titles, and your job titles all say "Speech-Language Pathologist," which is precisely the thing you're trying to reframe. A combination format lets you lead with the *translation* instead: who you are for this role, and the skills that prove it. By the time the recruiter reaches your clinical job titles, they already have the right frame.

Two specifics:

**Write a summary instead of an objective.** Objective statements ("Seeking a position where I can grow...") get roughly 28% fewer callbacks than value-focused summaries. An objective is about what you want. A summary is about what they get. Three lines, maximum, aimed at the target role. Save the farewell letter to your clinical career for your journal.

**Mirror the job description's exact vocabulary.** You're doing this for the skimming human, who is scanning for their own words. If the posting says "onboarding," don't write "orientation." If it says "stakeholders," don't write "team members and families." Their words, your evidence.

## How do you translate SLP experience into non-clinical language?

I didn't brainstorm these translations on a whiteboard. They come from SLPs who actually got hired into non-clinical roles: the phrasing that worked on real resumes, in real screens.

| What you did (clinical) | What it becomes (translated) |
|---|---|
| Students, IEPs, treatment plans | "Clinical projects" with goals, timelines, and deliverables |
| Caseload of 55 | "Managed a portfolio of 55 concurrent clients." **Keep the number.** |
| Teachers, admin, parents | Stakeholders |
| IEP meetings | Cross-functional stakeholder alignment |
| Therapy notes | Project documentation |
| Chart auditing | Detail-oriented quality assurance |
| Helped with the EMR transition | Training design + change management ("analyzed the documentation burden decrease after implementation") |
| Selling treatment plans to skeptical families | Persuasion, objection handling, closing |
| Translating jargon for parents | Plain-language communication, UX writing |
| Families | Customers: long-term relationship management and retention |

None of that middle column is a lie. You genuinely ran concurrent projects with deadlines and deliverables. You genuinely aligned people with competing agendas (if you've survived a contentious IEP meeting, corporate "stakeholder alignment" will feel like a spa day). Translation means describing work you already did in words the listener already understands. Spin would be inventing work. You're changing the labels.

The single most important habit inside these translations: **keep your numbers.** Companies want numbers. By how much did your project improve which variable? Caseload size, percentage of goals met, number of staff you trained, evaluations completed per month. Vague resumes feel like hiding. Specific ones feel like evidence.

## Should you just let AI write your resume?

Careful. This is where a lot of career changers are quietly torching their own applications in 2026.

The numbers are rough: 62% of employers say they reject AI-generated resumes that lack personalization. 33.5% of hiring managers say they can spot an AI-written resume in under 20 seconds. One in three. One hiring manager reported that 35% of applicants submitted *identical* AI-generated answers. Word. For. Word.

There's even a known tell-word list. If your resume says you're "adept" at "leveraging" skills, that you "spearheaded" initiatives with a "results-driven" mindset, a recruiter's eyes glaze before the second bullet. The words themselves are harmless. The recruiter has simply read them 200 times this week, in the same order, with no numbers attached.

The AI didn't sit in your IEP meetings. It doesn't know your caseload was 55, or that you trained 12 paraprofessionals, or that you were the only clinician the front office trusted with the EMR template. Generic AI output erases exactly the specifics that make you hireable. And the point of your resume is that no one else can claim your numbers.

Use tools, sure. I built one, and I'm about to mention it. But the rule is non-negotiable: **your real experience, your real numbers, translated. Never fabricated, never generic.**

## What do good before/after bullets look like?

Same experiences, before and after translation:

**Before:** *Managed caseload of 55 students; conducted evaluations and wrote IEPs.*
**After:** *Managed a portfolio of 55 concurrent clients, running each engagement through assessment, goal-setting, delivery, and documented outcome review.*

**Before:** *Attended IEP meetings with parents and teachers.*
**After:** *Led cross-functional stakeholder alignment meetings with families, educators, and administrators to negotiate goals and secure commitment to shared plans.*

**Before:** *Assisted with EMR rollout.*
**After:** *Designed and delivered training during an EMR transition, supporting staff adoption and analyzing the documentation burden decrease after implementation.*

**Before:** *Educated families on home practice strategies.*
**After:** *Translated technical findings into plain-language recommendations, driving follow-through from customers with zero clinical background.*

The "before" bullets describe duties in our dialect. The "after" bullets describe outcomes in theirs: verbs first, numbers kept, jargon gone.
