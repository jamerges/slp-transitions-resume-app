# SLP to Software Engineer: Jeannette Roberes on Being Qualified Before You Feel It

**The short answer:** Jeannette Roberes was a school-based SLP with a large caseload who opened a private practice, specialized in dyslexia, then completed a coding program in three months and worked as a software engineer for two and a half years. She now runs [Bearly Articulating](https://www.bearlyarticulating.com/), a disability-inclusion think tank founded in 2015, and wrote *Technical Difficulties: Why Dyslexic Narratives Matter in Tech*. Her line about the whole arc: **"People don't realize how qualified they truly are."**

**Takeaways:**

- The specific belief that kept her out of tech for years, and how wrong it turned out to be.
- Why she asked "what's the best thing that could happen?" instead of calculating the risk.
- Three months. That's how long the coding program took.
- Why she left software engineering after two and a half years, and why that isn't a failure.
- The distinction between *access* and *accessibility* that should change how you evaluate any employer.

---

Roberes started where a lot of this starts: a school-based SLP with a caseload big enough that the work followed her home. "I was happy with the work that I was doing. I however found that I was spending a lot of my weekends still doing work" while raising a young son, two years into a marriage.

She opened a private practice she named Bearly Articulating (the bear pun is intentional; her Instagram handle is @bearly_articulating). Dyslexia kept surfacing in the district, so she went deep: certifications, intervention programs. That specialization is what brought parents to her door. Then her marriage ended, three years into the practice, and she decided to reinvent herself.

## What kept an SLP out of tech for years?

A belief, and a common one. As an undergrad and grad student, Roberes says she "wouldn't dare look into technology, because that would mean math. That would mean engineering and science." Those were subjects this "little liberal arts person" was certain she couldn't grasp. She names the stigma directly: you have to be some kind of Jimmy Neutron or Dexter's Laboratory to join the tech workforce. A Brainiac. Someone categorically different from you.

That belief cost her years. It may be doing the same work on you right now: the quiet certainty that the interesting rooms are for other people.

## How did she actually decide to do it?

With a reframe. A coding bootcamp ad appeared in her search results, and instead of listing what could go wrong, she asked **"what's the best thing that could happen here?"**

Two outcomes, and she was fine with both: get accepted and learn to build websites and applications, or don't, and keep working as an SLP, "navigating caseload." She completed the program in three months.

## What did she do with it?

Software engineer, about two and a half years. Then she left, and her reason is more useful than a success story would have been: "the rigor of that work wasn't just the best for me, because I'm like a free spirit, carefree kind of gal, and that was stifling all of my creativity." The daily texture of the job didn't suit her, so she pivoted again, into presenting and facilitating trainings on how people with language-based learning disorders bring essential skills into the tech workforce.

Then the book, *Technical Difficulties: Why Dyslexic Narratives Matter in Tech*, published under the name Jeannette Washington. Bearly Articulating, the practice she'd started years earlier, became the umbrella for what came next: consulting, speaking in more than 40 countries, and training companies on inclusive tech. Her work with dyslexic learners has since earned coverage in *The Washington Post*, alongside the co-founder of Yale's Center for Dyslexia & Creativity, and in *U.S. News & World Report*. She's also a LETRS-certified facilitator through the International Dyslexia Association, literacy-instruction credentials layered on top of the software background and part of what makes her dyslexia consulting distinct from a purely clinical or purely technical take.

## Why does dyslexia matter in technology?

Because of who's in the room when the product gets made. "When technology is built one-size-fits-all, it becomes inaccessible to those who may be neurodiverse," Roberes argues. More people in tech who think, look, and interact with software differently means more people building for how users actually differ.

Her examples are concrete: bright colors that strain low vision, apps that let you read words but not hear them. Her challenge to anyone building anything: "Whose voice is the loudest when we are creating a product? Whose voice is the lowest?" On strengths, she points to big-picture thinking, episodic memory, and the kind of innovation dyslexic professionals bring to technical work that neurotypical colleagues often don't.

## Access vs. accessibility

> "Access invites you to pull a seat up to the table, while accessibility welcomes you to engage in the dialogue at the table."

Use it as a filter on your next job search. Plenty of organizations will give you access: an interview, an offer, a seat. Fewer are built so your particular way of working is actually usable once you're there.

## What she'd tell you about hiring, and about yourself

Roberes consults on inclusivity, and her most-repeated complaint from companies runs some version of: *we'd love to hire more autistic people, or more people with ADHD, but we don't know where to find them.*

Her answer is that it's a pipeline problem. If a company wants a specific group and can't recruit them, the recruitment phase is the place to look: referrals only from current employees, postings on one site, channels that group doesn't use. "If the job posting itself is inaccessible, then they just don't have access to that job."

Which loops back to her own story, and to yours. She had the capability the whole decade she spent outside tech. Nobody told her she was qualified, and she believed them.

**People don't realize how qualified they truly are.** If you've read this far, that probably includes you.
