# SLP to Software Engineer: Jeannette Roberes on Being Qualified Before You Feel It

**The short answer:** Jeannette Roberes was a school-based SLP with a large caseload who opened a private practice, specialized in dyslexia, then completed a coding program in three months and worked as a software engineer for two and a half years. She now speaks on how clinicians and dyslexic thinkers belong in technology, and wrote *Technical Difficulties: Why Dyslexic Narratives Matter in Tech*. Her line about the whole thing: **"People don't realize how qualified they truly are."**

**Takeaways:**

- The specific belief that kept her out of tech for years, and how wrong it turned out to be.
- Why she asked "what's the best thing that could happen?" instead of calculating the risk.
- Three months. That's how long the coding program took.
- Why she left software engineering after two and a half years — and why that isn't a failure.
- The distinction between *access* and *accessibility* that should change how you evaluate any employer.

---

Roberes started where a lot of this starts: a school-based SLP with a caseload big enough that the work followed her home. She was fine with the work itself. She was less fine with spending weekends on it, with a young child and a marriage two years in.

So she looked at her options and opened a private practice. Dyslexia kept coming up in the school district, so she went deep on it — certifications, intervention programs — and that specialization is what brought parents to her door.

Then her marriage ended. And she decided to reinvent herself.

## What kept an SLP out of tech for years?

A belief, and it's a common one.

Roberes says that as an undergrad and a grad student she wouldn't have gone near technology, because technology meant math, engineering, and science — things this "little liberal arts person" was certain she couldn't grasp.

She names the stigma directly: that you have to be some kind of Jimmy Neutron or Dexter's Laboratory to join the tech workforce. A Brainiac. Someone categorically different from you.

That belief cost her years. It's worth sitting with, because it's the same belief doing the same work on a lot of SLPs reading this — the quiet certainty that the interesting rooms are for other people.

## How did she actually decide to do it?

Not with a risk calculation. With a reframe.

A coding bootcamp ad appeared in her search results. Her question wasn't *what could go wrong* — it was **what's the best thing that could happen here?**

Two outcomes, and she was fine with both. She gets accepted and learns to build websites and applications. Or she doesn't, and she keeps working as an SLP navigating her caseload.

That's a small mental move with a large effect. Most transition paralysis comes from running the downside repeatedly and never pricing the upside. Roberes priced the upside, noticed the floor was "my current life continues," and applied.

She completed the program in **three months**.

## What did she do with it?

She worked as a software engineer for about two and a half years.

Then she left — and her reason is more useful than a success story would have been. She describes the rigor of that work as not the best fit for her, because she's a free spirit and it was stifling her creativity.

Notice what she did *not* say. She didn't say she couldn't do it, or that she'd made a mistake, or that the three months were wasted. She said the daily texture of the job didn't suit her, and she pivoted again.

That's the part people miss when they treat a career change as a one-way door. Roberes now had something rarer than either credential alone: a clinician who can actually build software, and speak to both rooms. She started presenting and facilitating trainings on how people with language-based learning disorders bring essential skills into the tech workforce — what websites should look like, where app accessibility helps and where it fails.

Then she wrote the book. Then she started building an app of her own.

## Why does dyslexia matter in technology?

Because of who's in the room when the product gets made.

Roberes' argument is that when technology is built one-size-fits-all, it becomes inaccessible to people who are neurodivergent. More people in tech who *think* differently, *look* at things differently, and *interact with software* differently means technology that meets more people where they are.

Her examples are concrete: bright colors that strain low vision, apps that let you read words but not hear them. And her challenge to anyone building anything is a question worth stealing — **whose voice is loudest when we create a product, and whose is quietest?**

On the strengths side, she points to big-picture thinking, episodic memory, and innovation that dyslexic professionals bring to technical work.

## Access vs. accessibility

This is the line from her book worth writing down:

> Access invites you to pull a seat up to the table. Accessibility welcomes you to engage in the dialogue at the table.

Use it as a filter when you're evaluating an employer during a transition. Plenty of organizations will give you access — an interview, an offer, a seat. Fewer are built so that once you're there, your particular way of working is actually usable.

## What she'd tell you about hiring, and about yourself

Roberes consults on inclusivity, and her most-repeated complaint from companies is some version of *we'd love to hire more autistic people, or more people with ADHD, but we don't know where to find them.*

Her answer is that it's a pipeline problem. If you say you want a specific group and you can't recruit them, look at the recruitment phase — are you only using referrals from current employees, only posting to one site, only reaching places that group isn't? If the job posting itself is inaccessible, those candidates simply don't have access to the job.

Which loops back to her own story, and to yours. The reason she wasn't in tech for a decade wasn't capability. It was that nobody told her she was qualified, and she believed them.

She was qualified the whole time.

**People don't realize how qualified they truly are.** If you've read this far, that probably includes you.
