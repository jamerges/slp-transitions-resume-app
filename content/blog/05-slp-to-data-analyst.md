Real people. Real outcomes. And a path almost no one mentions when SLPs ask "what else could I even do?"

And here's the part that took me embarrassingly long to see: you're already doing the job. You've just been doing it in a soundproof therapy room where nobody calls it analytics.

## What does a data analyst actually do all day?

A data analyst answers questions with numbers. That's the whole job description, de-jargoned.

Someone in the organization asks something like: *Did our falls-prevention program actually reduce falls? Which patients aren't showing up to discharge follow-ups, and why?* The analyst finds the relevant data, cleans it up (data is always messier than anyone admits), looks for the honest answer, and then. This is the underrated part, **explains it to people who don't speak numbers** so they can make a decision.

Day to day, that means:

- Pulling data from databases and EMRs (SQL is the standard tool — closer to advanced Excel than to real programming)
- Cleaning messy, inconsistent records into something usable
- Building charts and dashboards that make a pattern visible
- Presenting findings to clinical and administrative teams
- Documenting how you got the answer, so it holds up when someone pushes back

Notice what's *not* on the list: advanced math. The hard skills are learnable in months. The rare skill is judgment — knowing which questions matter and communicating answers to humans. Which brings me to you.

## Do SLPs actually have data skills?

You take session data every single day. Percent accuracy across trials, prompted versus independent, progress monitored against a baseline, targets adjusted when the numbers say the plan isn't working.

That is not "sort of like" data analysis. That **is** data analysis — collection, tracking against a goal, and decision-making based on what the numbers show. You've run an unbroken daily data practice for your entire career. Most career changers into this field are starting from zero on exactly the habit you never got to put on a resume.

The translation, in phrasing that works:

- Progress monitoring → **"collected, tracked, and analyzed performance data to drive treatment decisions"**
- Presenting at IEP or team meetings → **"presented outcome data to interdisciplinary teams and translated it into treatment decisions"**. That sentence beats "strong communication skills" in an interview every single time, because it's specific
- Chart auditing → **detail-oriented quality assurance**
- Explaining data to families → **plain-language reporting for non-technical stakeholders** — genuinely the hardest thing to teach analysts, and you've done it thousands of times

What you're missing is only the toolset: SQL, spreadsheets-at-a-higher-level, a dashboard tool. Learnable. The clinical judgment underneath is the part that isn't.

## How long does it really take? (The honest part)

Here's where I refuse to do the toxic-positivity thing.

That 500+ application, six-month grind? It really happened, to a qualified person, and it ended well. Across the SLP career-change community, transitions typically run **6-15 months**. This path can sit at the longer end if you do it the hard way.

The hard way is firing generic applications into job boards. That's the documented failure mode. And no wonder, when a single opening now averages 242 applicants. What actually shortens the road:

- **Referrals.** Most successful transitions run through a human connection, not a job board. Informational interviews aren't a nice-to-have on this path; they're the path.
- **Tailoring.** Mirror the job description's exact vocabulary. If the posting says "data visualization," your resume says "data visualization," not "made charts."
- **Speed.** Applications in the first day or two of a posting are the ones that get seen. Set alerts, apply fast.

Same destination. Very different number of applications.

## Where should you apply — and do you need a degree?

Here's the mistake that burns most career changers: aiming at big tech. Skip it. You'd be anonymous applicant #241 with no industry edge.

**The entry point is healthcare organizations.** Hospitals, health systems, behavioral health orgs — analyzing falls data, discharge follow-up rates, program outcomes. These employers need analysts who understand clinical context, care teams, and documentation reality. A business-school grad has to be taught what a discharge summary is. You've written hundreds. In healthcare analytics, your clinical years are the moat, not the gap.

On credentials, two documented routes:

- **The certificate route:** Google Data Analytics certificate, or DataCamp's learning path. Low cost, self-paced, and it doubles as your answer to the "prove you're serious" question every career-changer gets asked.
- **The degree route:** one SLP did a one-year online master's and landed the behavioral health analyst role. Real, but the expensive path. I'd exhaust the certificate route first and let a future employer help fund anything bigger.

No universal requirement for a new degree. What's required is proof of investment plus clinical experience translated into analyst language.

## What convinces a hiring manager you're actually serious?

Because here's the uncomfortable truth about career-changer rejections: they're rarely about ability. When hiring managers pass on someone like you, it's usually one of four unspoken doubts.

**"Will they bail back to clinical?"** Retention doubt is real and nobody says it out loud. Your answer is framing: never lead with burnout, even though the burnout is real. Frame pull, not push — what draws you to analysis (and for you it's honest: you already love the data part; it's the part of clinical work you'd keep).

**"I can't map these skills to my job."** This one's on the resume, not on you. "Provided skilled speech-language services" means nothing to an analytics manager. "Collected and analyzed performance data across a portfolio of 50 concurrent clients to drive treatment decisions" — same career, suddenly legible. If the interviewer has to do the translation themselves, they won't.

**"Where's the proof of investment?"** A certificate in progress, a written-up data story, informational interviews you can reference — any of these works. Zero of these is what sinks people.

**"Will they be unhappy with the money?"** Easier on this path than most, since the documented outcomes ($20k *more* than SLP base, in one case) mean you can honestly say the math works in your favor.

Then, in the interview itself, use the bridge structure that works across every SLP transition: what prompted the change framed as pull-toward, evidence of preparation, and two or three accomplishments mapped onto the problems this specific role faces in its first 90 days. If the job posting mentions falls dashboards, your accomplishment about tracking and presenting outcome data is the one you tell.

Specific beats impressive. Every time.

## What should you do this month?

**1. Start the Google Data Analytics certificate (or DataCamp).** Don't finish it this month — just start, and put "in progress" on LinkedIn. Proof of investment starts counting immediately.

**2. Write your data story in analyst language.** Take one real clinical win — a client whose data made you change course, an outcome you presented to a team. And write it as: question, data, analysis, decision, result. That's a portfolio piece and an interview answer in one.

**3. Set alerts for healthcare analyst roles and find two humans.** Alerts: "data analyst" plus hospital systems and behavioral health orgs in your region (plus remote). Humans: LinkedIn search "SLP data analyst" or "speech pathologist analyst". The people from the stories above exist, and most remember exactly how lonely the start was.
