# What Health-Tech Founders Actually Need From SLPs: Gareth Walkom and withVR

**The short answer:** Gareth Walkom has stuttered since he was six. He studied digital media technology, then medical product design, then built [withVR](https://withvr.app): virtual reality speaking situations that a speech therapist controls in real time from a laptop while the client wears a headset. He has never been an SLP and doesn't claim to be. He founded the company on International Stuttering Awareness Day in 2020 with his own savings, and the product now runs in clinics, university labs, and at least one NHS trust. The clinical judgment inside it came from somewhere else: the hundred-plus clinicians and researchers he built it with.

**Takeaways:**

- Why the person who built this speech therapy tool isn't a speech therapist, and why the distinction works in your favor.
- The design decision that determines whether clinicians adopt a tool or quietly stop using it after week three.
- What the published research on VR for stuttering actually supports right now, stated carefully.
- Five concrete ways SLPs plug into companies like this, from scenario design to service implementation.
- The one thing a founder can't get anywhere except from a clinician.

---

Walkom describes stuttering as something that shaped more than his speech. "This also really affects you not just through your speech, but also mentally and emotionally as well," he told James on the Xceptional Leaders podcast.

His degree was a BSc in digital media technology at Nottingham Trent University, a course built around making games for teaching, learning, and training, with virtual reality threaded through it. He followed it with an MSc in medical product design, where he paired an eye-tracking VR headset with software that could measure what a person did with their eyes inside a virtual scene. Along the way he began treating his own condition as the design brief.

He is careful about how that happened. "It's not something that I thought about at the start," he said of the clinical direction. "It's just something that shapes itself." He built a prototype, saw what it did, and followed it.

According to the company's own account, he incorporated withVR BV on 22 October 2020, International Stuttering Awareness Day, funding it from personal savings while holding a PhD grant at KU Leuven. He chose to ship the tool rather than wait several years to finish the doctorate first.

## What is VR exposure therapy, in plain terms?

Two pieces, stacked. Exposure therapy means gradually introducing someone to a situation they fear, whether that's public speaking, spiders, or heights. Virtual reality moves that situation into a scene the clinician can build.

Walkom's summary of why the combination works: "It's not real inside [virtual reality], but it does feel real." The advantage over the real café is that you can stop it. A situation that would otherwise have to be survived can be paused, dialed down, and restarted.

He splits the technology into two pillars. Video VR uses recorded 180- or 360-degree footage, which looks convincing and provokes motion sickness in a lot of people, and which you cannot change once it's shot. The other pillar is 3D models and avatars, marginally less photoreal, and adjustable while the session is running. That second pillar is what withVR is built on, because the whole clinical value sits in the adjusting.

## Why did a technologist build the thing clinicians couldn't measure?

The eye-tracking work started with a gap Walkom noticed in how his own stuttering was assessed. People who stutter sometimes blink, flicker, or fixate during a moment of stuttering. Those behaviors get logged subjectively, in a clinician's impression, because there's no practical way to count them live. "It's very hard to write down an exact number," he said.

So he went after the number. The research configuration he described uses headsets with eye tracking plus face cameras, heart-rate sensors, and a measure of cognitive load, all recording while the person speaks inside the scene.

That measure of cognitive load leads somewhere clinically interesting. He described it as a 0-to-100 scale where the goal is not the top. Performance peaks somewhere in the middle of the distress range, where a person feels stretched rather than flooded, and the sensor data tells the therapist when a client has tipped past it. SLPs already work this territory by feel, reading a kid's face and backing off. James calls it the goldilocks stress zone. Walkom's pitch is that a machine can help you find its edges faster.

## Who holds the controls?

The clinician, and Walkom is emphatic about it. "We provide a toolbox which is controlled by a speech therapist," he said. The therapist works from a dashboard on a laptop, sees exactly what the client sees inside the headset, and changes the speaking situation while it runs. There's an autopilot mode for scenarios programmed in advance, and the client can drive their own progression through tasks.

Ask him what's adjustable and the answer gets granular. Number of people in the scene, what they look like, whether they react warmly or coldly, and the noise: "the noise of the people, the noise of the music, the coffee machine making a really loud noise." A café queue holds a dozen variables, and the one that undoes a particular client might be the line forming behind them while the barista never registers at all.

Cueing works the same way. A hand signal, a verbal prompt, a light: whatever a therapist already does in the room gets mapped into the scene as a color shift or a dimming. Walkom then names the limit that keeps the tool honest: "In a real life speaking situation, the speech therapist won't always be there." The cues are scaffolding to remove.

The product, as of this writing, offers a dozen-plus environments including a café, classroom, bakery, supermarket, meeting room, and auditorium, with AI-generated voices in more than 50 languages. Public pricing starts at €49 per month for an individual license, with a €20 web-only tier for clinicians without a headset, and a Meta Quest running roughly €350 on top.

## What does the evidence actually show?

Less than the marketing around VR in health care generally implies, and enough to keep going.

The best-known independent trial in this specific area is a pilot randomized controlled study from Imperial College London, published in Frontiers in Digital Health in 2023, unconnected to withVR. Twenty-five adults who stutter were randomized to smartphone-delivered VR exposure therapy or a waitlist. It did not show the intervention beating waitlist on social anxiety or speech-related distress immediately after treatment, though improvements appeared at one-month follow-up. The authors flag the small sample, the attrition, the cheap headsets, and the lack of personalization.

withVR's own research footprint has grown since the podcast. A 2025 pilot study in the Journal of Voice, co-authored by Walkom with a Ghent University team, used Therapy withVR with eleven trans women in gender-affirming voice training and reported gains in fundamental frequency and willingness to communicate, with modest change in voice-related quality of life. Eleven participants. The authors ask for larger and longer studies, which is the correct thing to ask for.

At Michigan State, Bridget Walsh's NIH-funded Developmental Speech Lab worked with Walkom to build two conditions for preschoolers: a higher-load virtual classroom circle and a lower-load conversation with a virtual pet. That work was still recruiting when it was written up in 2024.

Anyone selling you certainty about VR outcomes in stuttering right now is ahead of the literature. Walkom's own framing on the podcast was closer to the truth of it: the sensors are in the tools, they're being tested with therapists and researchers, and the results so far are encouraging.

## What do founders like this actually need from SLPs?

Walkom could build the renderer, the eye tracker, and the dashboard. He could not, from his own experience alone, know what a fluency shaping session looks like in week six, or which cue a school-based clinician would actually use, or how a scenario should differ for a nine-year-old and a job seeker. The company says it has built the tool alongside 100-plus clinicians and researchers across 30 countries. That is the clinical layer of a software product, built by people with caseloads.

The roles that layer breaks into are real jobs:

**Scenario and content design.** Someone has to specify what the barista says, how the classroom is arranged, what a graded hierarchy looks like for selective mutism versus dysarthria. That is clinical reasoning rendered as a spec.

**Research collaboration.** The Ghent voice study and the MSU preschool work both ran through academic SLPs who brought the question, the protocol, and the participants. The company brought the software.

**Implementation inside services.** In November 2023, the Barnsley children's and adults' speech and language therapy team at South West Yorkshire Partnership NHS Foundation Trust became the first NHS trust to adopt the software, funded by the trust's charity for two headsets. Speech and language therapist Nicola Maddy described the point of it as bridging "the gap between speaking within the safety of the clinic environment and the real world." Getting a tool from purchase order to routine use is a clinician's job, and it's the job most tech companies underestimate.

**Clinical advisory.** Part-time, paid, often a few hours a month, and the most common first paid non-clinical work SLPs pick up. It exists because a founder who ships an unusable feature burns a quarter.

**Customer-facing roles.** Onboarding, training, and support for a product whose users are clinicians tends to go badly when staffed by people who have never run a session.

The through line: the founder owns the technology and the lived experience, and buys clinical judgment from people who have it. If you're an SLP wondering what health tech would want from you, that's the answer. Nobody is asking you to sit through a coding bootcamp.

## Where this leaves you

Walkom's route into speech therapy technology is not one an SLP can copy, and pretending otherwise would waste your time. He came in from the user's side of the table with an engineering degree and a problem he'd lived since childhood.

The useful part is what he needed once he got here. Every clinical assumption inside that product was supplied by somebody with a caseload. Some of them collaborated for free, some got authorship, some got contracts, and a few now work in the industry full time. The distance between those groups is mostly a matter of who asked.

His closing offer on the episode was an open door, and it still describes how these relationships tend to start: get in touch, try it, tell us what's wrong with it. "You can even start without a virtual reality headset."

You already know what happens in the room. That's the scarce input.
