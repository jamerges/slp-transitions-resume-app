# SLP to Trauma-Informed Care Consultant: Rachel Archambault on Building a Niche From What You Survived

**The short answer:** In her first year as a fully credentialed SLP, Rachel Archambault's high school went through a traumatic event that drew national coverage. Searching online for how to support her students afterward, she found trauma-informed care. A year later, sitting in a hotel room on a family vacation, she made an Instagram account called [the PTSD SLP](https://ptsdslp.com) and started posting what she'd learned. It became a consulting practice that now takes her around the country training SLPs, doctors, PTs, OTs, schools, hospitals, and universities.

**Takeaways:**

- The six pillars she teaches, and why she treats them as a checklist rather than a sequence.
- The language changes she made that cost nothing and changed the room.
- Where the line sits between supporting mental health and overstepping into therapy. She is emphatic about it.
- Why she rewrote her own signature talk to remove her story, and what that says about knowing your audience.
- How a niche practice grows from a thing you needed and couldn't find.

---

Archambault was in her second year as a speech pathologist, her first fully credentialed, working at a high school. The event that happened there was violent, and it was covered nationally. She has made a deliberate choice not to retell it in her presentations, so this article won't either.

Her business grew out of what came after. She was a young clinician with her own trauma, inside a community with its own, responsible for students with theirs. "I could not care about the students that were in front of me trying to work on an R sound when they didn't feel safe on campus," she says.

The support arrived at scale and landed badly. Hers is the sixth-largest school district in the country, and school psychologists were deployed from across it, pulling resources from other schools. But the counselors were all put in the library, so students sat next to each other recounting their stories within earshot. The next day that counselor was gone and a student had to start over with someone new.

So she typed a question into a search engine: how do you work with students who have been traumatized? The answer that came back was trauma-informed care, a framework she discovered she'd been partly practicing without a name for it.

## What is trauma-informed care, and what isn't it?

Archambault is firm on the boundary, because it's the first objection she hears. SLPs tell her *I'm not a therapist. I'm not a psychologist.*

Correct, she says. "Our job is not to treat the trauma. That's not what we're doing." What an SLP can do is avoid causing harm and refer to the right professional. She's blunt about the failure mode: she's met clinicians handed the ACEs questionnaire who now believe their job is to investigate what traumas a student has sustained. Investigating belongs to the school psychologist.

The six pillars she teaches are safety, choice, collaboration, trust, mutuality, and cultural and historical awareness. She runs them as a checklist: *am I providing safety to this client? am I providing choice?* There is no ladder, and no order to climb.

Safety comes first for her, with a correction most clinicians need. "I would rather say I am not the safe person," she says, against the instinct to think of yourself as a student's chosen adult. The person in the room decides whether it is safe, with their body and their nervous system, whatever you announce. What you can do is ask: what would make you feel more safe here? And if a student can't answer, work backward. Find what in the room is distressing them, or leave the room and take the session on a walk.

## The language changes

The changes she likes best cost nothing.

After the event, her school stopped saying *shoot me a text* and *shoot me an email*. Nobody trained them to. It simply stopped, for the six years she was there, replaced with *send me a message*. The reasoning generalizes past that one school: a violent turn of phrase can pull someone out of their learning brain and into survival brain, and you rarely know who in the room needs that not to happen.

Her argument against selective care is practical. If you tiptoe only around the kids whose histories you know, you'll miss the ones whose situations changed overnight. Remove the violent language across the board and you cover everyone. She notes the same applies in adult settings, and especially in veterans' care.

She also flags *dear parents* on school letters, which she'd change to *dear adults*, since plenty of children live with a grandparent, a sister, an uncle.

And she has no patience for the reflex to make it better with optimism. After her own experience she heard *everything will be okay*, *you're so strong*, and the one that lands worst, *other people have it worse.* "It's dismissive of our feelings," she says. Someone will always have it worse; that doesn't unmake what the person in front of you is feeling. The evidence-based alternative is to validate rather than redirect: *that sounds really hard. Thank you for telling me.*

## Why she took her own story out of her talk

Her first presentation was a two-hour session on a Florida Speech-Language Hearing Association cruise in June 2019. She was asked to recount what happened at her school, and she did.

She wouldn't now. "I find that especially to SLPs that work in the system, this is a very scary, real fear that they have, that this is going to happen to me." An audience bracing for what they're about to hear isn't an audience that can absorb a framework. She'll still tell someone privately at a convention if they ask.

There's a craft lesson buried in a decision that was really an ethical one. The origin story is what makes the expertise credible, and it's also the thing that can make the room unusable. She kept the credibility and dropped the retelling.

## How does a niche practice actually start?

Impulsively, in her case. She describes herself as an impulsive person generally, and the Instagram account was created in a hotel room in Nashville on family vacation, a thought she acted on immediately rather than planned.

What made it grow was that the resources were real. Her school had received support from experts flown in from around the country after a nationally recognized event. Almost no other SLP had access to that material. She posted it for clinicians dealing with trauma in their own settings, and it "kind of become this juggernaut."

Then the market moved toward her. Coming out of COVID, schools and practices recognized a mental health problem and started asking what to do about it. Her bookings grew over the following two years: Indiana, Georgia, around the country. At ASHA there's now a trauma-informed track.

She's generous about the crowd: more SLPs are presenting on this than when she started, each through the lens of their own specialty, whether AAC or feeding. She talks about it more generally, which turns out to be its own niche.

## What she'd tell an SLP thinking about consulting

Asked for advice for aspiring entrepreneurs, she talked about how you treat the people who work for you.

"You have to be more trauma-informed than the person who is under you." Her administrators need to be more so than she is, so it can pass down to the students. She points at policy as the place it becomes concrete: a school with two sick days that permits attending a wedding only for immediate family. What about an only child whose best friend is like a brother? "Who does this leave out? Who would this hurt?"

The reflection she asks for is uncomfortable on purpose. Most people will say they've never caused harm. Working backward from the assumption that you have is how you find the systems that do it quietly.

## What to take from this if you're still in the clinic

Archambault's business came from a need she had and couldn't fill, in a situation she didn't choose. Nobody can plan for that. The mechanics underneath it, though, repeat.

She had material nobody else had, and she gave it away publicly before she had any idea it was a business. She built the audience on a free platform a year before there was an offer. She learned her audience well enough to rebuild her own talk around what they could hear. And she stayed rigorously inside her scope, which is what makes clinicians trust her.

If you're sitting on knowledge your colleagues keep asking you about, you're in the same starting position. The account is free.
