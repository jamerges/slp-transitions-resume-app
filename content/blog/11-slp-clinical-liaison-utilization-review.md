# SLP to Clinical Liaison or Utilization Review: The Fastest Ways Out

Most "leave the field" advice comes with a homework assignment.

Learn to code. Build a portfolio. Get the Google cert, then the other cert, then network for a year while your current caseload quietly eats you alive. And look — those paths are real, I write about them constantly. But there's a whole category of exits that gets skipped because it isn't shiny, and it happens to be the fastest one:

**The roles where your clinical credential is already the qualification.**

No bootcamp. No cert ladder. No nights teaching yourself Python while your cat judges you. Clinical liaison and utilization review are the two doors that are essentially already unlocked for licensed SLPs — and today I want to walk through what's actually behind each one, who thrives there, and the honest catch.

## What does a clinical liaison actually do all day?

Clinical liaison (sometimes "rehab liaison") has been called **the #1 non-clinical title for rehab professionals** — and the pay range backs it up: **$84k to $135k**, which for a lot of SLPs is a raise on day one.

Here's the shape of the job: you're the clinical evaluator and educator who sits between a rehab hospital and the places its patients come from. Day to day, that looks like reviewing referrals, assessing whether a patient is appropriate for a rehab admission, explaining what rehab can offer to families and case managers, and building relationships with the acute-care teams who send patients your way.

Notice what's in that sentence. Assessing patients. Explaining complicated clinical realities to stressed families. Coordinating with case managers and physicians. You have done every single one of those things — you just did them while also carrying a caseload, writing the notes, and running the sessions.

The liaison role keeps the parts of clinical work many of us actually liked — the assessment brain, the family conversations, the "here's what's possible" moments — and removes the productivity-tracked treatment grind.

The employers to know: **Encompass Health, Select Medical, and Lifepoint** are the big rehab systems that hire these roles constantly. Search "clinical liaison" or "rehab liaison" on their careers pages directly, not just on the job boards.

One clarification, because it comes up: if you've heard of pharma **Medical Science Liaison** roles and gotten excited — pump the brakes. MSL positions are effectively doctorate-gated (PhD, PharmD, MD). Don't spend a year chasing that door. Clinical liaison is the accessible cousin, and it's hiring.

## What does utilization review actually involve?

If clinical liaison is the extrovert's fast exit, utilization review is the introvert's.

UR is the work of reviewing clinical documentation against medical-necessity criteria — is this admission justified, is this length of stay supported, does the chart back up the care being billed? You're the person who reads the record with a clinician's eye and makes the call, or builds the case for the person who does.

The honest pitch: **$80-88k ($37-62/hr), remote-heavy, and fast entry.** For SLPs, "remote-heavy" is often the headline. This is one of the few clinical-credential jobs where working from your kitchen table is normal rather than a unicorn posting.

And if you've ever done chart audits — or been on the receiving end of a denial and thought *I could write a better justification than this* — you already understand the core skill. Years of writing documentation designed to survive review means you know exactly what reviewers look for. Now you'd be the reviewer.

Within the same family, there's a quieter option worth knowing: **state Disability Determination Services** — the agencies that evaluate disability claims. One SLP who made that move took a **$13k pay cut** to do it. Why would anyone do that? Two words: mobility and PSLF. State employment came with public-service loan forgiveness and a career ladder out of direct care. I'm telling you the pay cut number on purpose, because the tradeoffs are real and you deserve the actual math, not a highlight reel. For an SLP carrying heavy grad school debt, loan forgiveness can quietly outweigh the salary difference. For someone debt-free, it probably doesn't.

## Who thrives in these roles — and who doesn't?

Here's my honest read.

**You'll probably thrive if:** you like the clinical *world* but not the clinical *caseload*. You enjoy the puzzle of a chart, the family conversation, the interdisciplinary chess match — and what's burning you out is the productivity math, the paperwork-after-hours, the schedule you don't control. These jobs keep the medicine and drop the grind.

Liaison specifically rewards people who genuinely like talking to strangers — you're building referral relationships, which is relationship work all day. If selling a treatment plan to a skeptical family was secretly your favorite part of the job (persuasion, objection handling, closing — yes, that's what it was), liaison will feel like home.

UR rewards the opposite temperament: the chart-audit brain. Detail-oriented, criteria-driven, happy to work independently, allergic to small talk before 10 a.m. If your therapy notes were the tidiest in the building, UR is your lane.

**You might not thrive if:** what you actually want is *distance* from healthcare. And that matters enough to get its own section.

## What's the catch?

Here it is, plainly: **you stay adjacent to the system you're leaving.**

Same hospitals. Same insurance logic. Same acronyms. You're on the other side of the desk now, but it's the same desk.

For some SLPs, that's exactly right — the problem was never healthcare, it was the caseload, the red tape, the Sunday night scaries about Monday's schedule. Change the seat, keep the world, and the job gets good again.

For others, these roles are a halfway house. A genuinely useful one! You can take a liaison or UR job, catch your breath at $84k+ without a single certification, and *then* decide whether you want to keep going toward tech, data, or something further from a hospital badge. There's no rule that your first move has to be your last one. Honestly, a paid, lower-stress vantage point is a much better place to plan a second move than a burnt-out Sunday night — burnt-out brains genuinely struggle with the executive-function load of a career change. Reduce the burnout first; plan second.

Just know which one you're choosing, so you don't land in UR and wonder in month six why you still dread the word "denial."

## How do you actually make the first move?

Some tactical honesty, because "just apply!" is the kind of vague advice this site exists to replace:

**Where these jobs live.** For liaison roles, go directly to the rehab systems — Encompass Health, Select Medical, Lifepoint — plus the standard boards. For UR, search "utilization review" and "clinical reviewer" with your license type; many postings ask for "licensed clinician" broadly, and your SLP license qualifies. Set alerts, because **speed matters: applications in the first 1-2 days of a posting are the ones that get seen.**

**How to frame your experience.** Your dysphagia and rehab background is the asset — lead with it. A medical SLP has evaluated patients across acute and post-acute settings, made discharge recommendations, and defended clinical decisions to families and teams. On the resume, translate it: IEP-style team meetings become "cross-functional stakeholder alignment," chart auditing becomes "detail-oriented QA," and your caseload becomes "a portfolio of X concurrent clients" — keep the number.

**Frame pull, not push.** Never lead with burnout in an interview, even though it's real and even though they'd understand. The hiring side's quiet worry about clinicians is retention — *will they bail back to clinical?* The frame that works is a bridge: what's pulling you toward this role, the evidence you've prepared for it, and 2-3 accomplishments mapped onto the job's first-90-days problems. "I want to use my clinical judgment across a whole census instead of one caseload" lands. "I'm so done" does not — save that for your group chat.

**Use referrals if you possibly can.** Community reality check: referrals decide most successful transitions, and generic online applications without them are what fails. The good news is that for these specific roles, your referral network already exists — every case manager, rehab director, and liaison you've ever coordinated a discharge with is one message away.

## Is "fast" the same as "settling"?

One last thing, because I know how the inner voice works.

Somewhere in your head there might be a whisper that taking the clinical-adjacent exit is less impressive than teaching yourself data science. That whisper is wrong, and it's also not your friend. The typical career change takes 6-15 months of sustained effort — some take longer. A liaison or UR role can compress that to a single hiring cycle, pay you fairly while you get your evenings back, and leave every other door open.

You deserve to wake up and not dread going to work. The fastest honest route to that is allowed to be the one you take.
