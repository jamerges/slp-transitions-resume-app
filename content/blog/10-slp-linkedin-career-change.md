Picture the moment your application actually starts working.

A recruiter reads your carefully translated resume — the one where your caseload became a portfolio and your IEP meetings became cross-functional stakeholder alignment. They're intrigued. A clinician who gets it. So they do what recruiters do before reaching out to anyone: they look you up on LinkedIn.

And they find... "Speech-Language Pathologist | CCC-SLP." A headline from 2019. An About section about your passion for pediatric feeding. Skills topped by "Dysphagia" and "Articulation Therapy."

Now you have a worse problem than being unqualified. You look *inconsistent*. The polished non-clinical resume and the fully clinical profile don't describe the same person — and when a recruiter has to choose which version to believe, they believe LinkedIn. The resume starts to read like something a tool generated for you, which, in the era of AI-slop applications, is the fastest way to get quietly dropped. Remember: 62% of employers reject AI-generated application materials that lack personalization. A contradicting profile is exactly what makes yours look like one of them.

Your LinkedIn isn't a formality. It's the background check on your story. Let's make it corroborate you.

## Why does your LinkedIn matter more than you think?

Two reasons, and they work in opposite directions.

**Defense:** the consistency check I just described. Recruiters screen your profile before interviews, before callbacks, sometimes before deciding whether your resume gets a full read. If the profile contradicts the resume, the doubt lands on the resume — you've spent hours translating a document that your own profile is now undermining for free.

**Offense:** LinkedIn is a search engine, and recruiters are its power users. They search for the terms in the roles they're filling — "customer success," "implementation," "program coordinator," "learning and development." A profile built entirely from clinical vocabulary is invisible to every one of those searches. You're not being rejected. You're not being *found*, which feels identical from the inside.

A translated profile isn't just recruiter bait. It's the landing page for every networking conversation you'll have.

## What should your headline say instead of "SLP | CCC-SLP"?

Your headline follows you everywhere on LinkedIn — every comment, every connection request, every search result. It's the highest-value real estate you own, and most SLPs spend it restating their license.

Here's the thing: "SLP | CCC-SLP" is a perfect headline *for a clinical job search*. It's precise, credentialed, searchable — by people hiring clinicians. Which is the one audience you're trying to leave.

The formula for a career changer has two parts:

**Target-role keywords + bridge credibility.**

Target-role keywords are the words your future recruiter actually types: the role, the function, the domain. Bridge credibility is the compressed version of why you, specifically — the clinical background reframed as an asset for *that* role.

The shape, not a script (please don't copy-paste — sameness is the enemy):

- *Customer Success & Implementation | 7 Years Managing Client Outcomes in Healthcare | SLP Background*
- *Program Coordination | Cross-Functional Stakeholder Alignment | Clinician Turned Operations*
- *Learning & Development | Training Design + Plain-Language Communication | Healthcare Background*

Notice what these do. The recruiter's search terms come first. The clinical career isn't hidden — it's positioned as the differentiator at the end, not the identity at the front. And notice what's absent: no "aspiring," no "seeking opportunities," no "in transition." Those words read as apology. You're not aspiring to have your skills. You have them; you're renaming them.

## What should a career-changer's About section actually say?

The About section is where SLPs freeze, because it feels like it's asking for your memoir. It isn't. It's asking for three short paragraphs, written in first person, for one specific reader: **the recruiter searching the target role's terms.**

Not your grad school cohort. Not your former CF supervisor. The recruiter. Write for the person you want to find you, in the vocabulary they search, and the section practically outlines itself:

**Paragraph 1 — the bridge claim.** Who you are in target-role terms, and the one-line case for why the clinical background is an asset. The interview line that works out loud works here too: *"I've been doing customer success my whole career — I just called it patient and family care."* Adapt the shape to your target. Then back it with a number.

**Paragraph 2 — the evidence.** Two or three translated accomplishments with the numbers kept. Portfolio of 55 concurrent clients. Trained staff through an EMR transition and analyzed the documentation burden decrease afterward. Presented outcome data to interdisciplinary teams and translated it into decisions. Specificity beats labels — "strong communicator" is a claim; "translated technical findings into plain-language recommendations customers actually followed" is a receipt.

**Paragraph 3 — the pull.** Where you're heading and why it's a move *toward* something, plus your proof of investment in the new field — the cert, the course, the project. This is retention-doubt insurance: hiring managers quietly worry career changers will bail back to the old field, and burnout-flavored copy ("ready for a change after years of paperwork...") feeds that worry directly. Pull, not push. Every time.

First person throughout. Third-person LinkedIn summaries read like a press release for a company of one, and nobody has ever finished one.

## Should you rewrite your experience section too?

Yes — but you don't need to rewrite everything. Translate the top bullets under each role, and keep the numbers.

Take the same translations that fixed your resume and apply them to the two or three bullets a skimming recruiter will actually read: students and IEPs become clinical projects with goals, timelines, and deliverables. Teachers, admin, and parents become stakeholders. Chart auditing becomes detail-oriented QA. Selling treatment plans to skeptical families becomes persuasion and objection handling — which is exactly what it was, as anyone who has pitched a home-practice program to an exhausted parent at 4:45 on a Friday can confirm.

Your **skills section** needs the same treatment, and it's the five-minute fix with the best return: reorder it. LinkedIn lets you pin your top skills — and recruiters filter searches by them. If your top slots say "Dysphagia," "AAC," and "Early Intervention," you're optimized for the exact job you're leaving. Move the target-role skills up — project documentation, stakeholder management, training design, customer retention, whatever your target JD keeps repeating. Clinical skills can stay; they just shouldn't be the headliners anymore.

## What should you NOT do to your profile?

The temptation, once you commit to the pivot, is to scrub the clinician out entirely. Resist it. There's a line between reframing and rewriting history, and crossing it costs you the exact credibility you're trying to build.

**Don't lie.** Not about titles, not about dates, not about a "Program Manager" role that was actually a caseload. Your resume and profile will be compared line by line — consistency is the whole reason you're doing this — and fabrication is the one screening flag nobody recovers from.

**Don't delete your clinical history.** A profile with mysterious gaps where a career should be raises far more questions than a profile that says "clinician, translated." Your history isn't the problem; the framing is. And your background is genuinely a differentiator — at speech-tech and health-tech companies, the clinical credential itself is the qualification. Deleting it would be throwing away your rarest asset to look like a generic junior applicant, of which the market has plenty.

**Don't agonize over the Open to Work banner.** People debate the green ring endlessly — desperation signal or useful flag, recruiters disagree with each other. I won't pretend there's a verdict. There's also a recruiters-only setting if you'd rather not broadcast to your current employer. The banner is a coin flip; the headline, About, and skills are the levers. Spend your energy on the levers.

**Don't set it and abandon it.** A translated profile that shows zero activity since the rewrite looks staged. You don't need to become a daily poster — an occasional comment in the spaces where your target field talks is enough to look like a live human walking toward something.

## How do you actually start? (Without staring at a blank headline box)

Order of operations, for the executive-function-friendly version — because burnt-out brains genuinely struggle with planning, and this is not the season of life to freestyle it:

1. **Headline first.** Target-role keywords + bridge credibility. Ten minutes, biggest visibility gain.
2. **Skills reorder.** Five minutes. Target-role skills into the top slots.
3. **About section.** Three short first-person paragraphs: bridge claim, evidence with numbers, pull.
4. **Top experience bullets.** Translate the first two or three under each recent role.
