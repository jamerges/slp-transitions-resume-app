# How to Grow a Podcast While Working Full Time: Maya Chupkov on Building Proud Stutter

**The short answer:** Maya Chupkov is not an SLP. She's a woman who stutters, working in local news and media policy, who launched [Proud Stutter](https://www.proudstutter.org) on International Stuttering Awareness Day in October 2021 and turned it into an Ambie-nominated show, a run of coverage in NPR, The Guardian, ABC7 and USA Today, a nonprofit-sponsored advocacy project, and a feature documentary in production. She did all of it around a full-time job. She describes the mechanics: a publishing cadence she refused to break, a self-taught press routine, and the point at which she admitted she needed a team. It is the most concrete answer I've heard to "I want to start a podcast/newsletter/community but I don't know how anyone actually sustains one."

**Takeaways:**

- The cadence rule that did more for growth than any single piece of press, and why a bonus episode counts.
- Her exact method for finding reporters who might cover you, using nothing but Google's news tab.
- The "why" test a story has to pass before you pitch it to anyone.
- Why she was mediocre at PR for a decade and good at it the moment it was her own cause.
- The burnout that forced her to build a board, and what a clinician can learn from her own speech therapy.

---

Chupkov started stuttering around the time she started talking. Her first language was Hebrew; when teachers at nursery school couldn't understand her, her parents switched the household to English. Her mother noticed the stutter in elementary school, assumed she'd grow out of it the way many children do, and started paying attention when it was still there in middle school.

Fourth grade is the year she remembers most vividly, and she remembers it for the bullying. What followed was a decade and a half of hiding: a wall up at school, few close friends, a habit of routing around sounds and situations so precisely that most people never knew. I met her at UCSB in 2013, talked with her at a party, and had no idea. I was in the middle of a speech-language pathology degree at the time.

"I developed these habits and these weird tics to really hide my stutter," she said. "You wouldn't think it's a stutter, because that's me trying to navigate around it."

She carried it into college and past it, into therapy, the regular kind rather than speech therapy, where she still didn't bring it up. "It was so deep down there that I wouldn't even mention it."

## Why did she start the podcast in the first place?

The pandemic, and a job she hated. She was working at the California Public Utilities Commission, had walked in expecting one thing and found another, and was miserable. "I just needed something to help me get out of bed in the morning, something outside of work."

Podcasting felt accessible. She had no topic. Her husband suggested stuttering, which she had never once considered as a subject, because he had never treated it as a problem to be managed. That external read on her own speech is what made the idea thinkable.

Before launching, she ran a test. She told a group of people she sort of knew, and had always felt a strange distance from, that she stutters. "As soon as I opened up about it, I felt this sense of connection that I've never felt before."

"For so long I'd been saying you need to be authentic," she said, "but really, I was hiding the most personal part of myself."

Proud Stutter launched in October 2021 with an episode called "Our Coming Out Story," co-hosted for the first season with her friend Cynthia Chin.

## What does consistency actually look like?

Every other Friday. For two and a half years at the time of our conversation, with exactly one break.

She names it as one of the top two reasons the show worked. What makes it survivable alongside a full-time job: the every-other-Friday promise covers the slot, and the production value can flex. A short bonus episode rounding up highlights keeps the streak alive on a week when a full interview isn't happening.

She learned the slot mattered from the one time she skipped it. A handful of listeners emailed to ask where the episode was. They hadn't unsubscribed or complained; they'd built her into a Friday routine and noticed a hole in it.

Her husband had assumed the show would run one season, because how much is there to say about stuttering? By her third year she had the opposite problem. "You can literally connect stuttering to anything and have an interesting conversation." The show has since passed a hundred episodes.

## How do you get press with no budget and no contacts?

Chupkov fell into PR by accident after graduating from UC Santa Barbara: a random internship at one of the largest PR firms in the world, then several more agency and comms jobs, then grad school for public policy. Her routine now runs roughly like this.

**Start with the why.** Before contacting anyone, she asks what makes a reporter care. For Proud Stutter the answer never changed: stuttering is consistently misrepresented, and "we need more representation of what stuttering really is." Absent a reason the story is different from the one that outlet already ran, there's nothing to pitch.

**Find reporters through Google, not LinkedIn.** She types keywords into Google's news tab, filters to roughly the last six months, and reads what comes back. When an article is adjacent to her issue, she clicks through to the byline and looks for a pattern in what that person covers. Reporters who cover your thing repeatedly go on a list. Twitter used to work for this and mostly doesn't now; LinkedIn was never much use, because a lot of reporters keep their profiles private.

**For TV, search the station, not the network.** She looked through her local station's site for recurring segments and found one built around feel-good stories. Accepting your stutter fits a feel-good segment. She emailed that specific reporter, named the segment, pitched an idea sized for it, and got on the show.

**Then follow up like it's the job, because it is.** "You have to follow up, you have to really study a reporter and really understand what they want." In a [Medium post on PR for indie podcasters](https://medium.com/@proudstutter/pr-tips-for-indie-podcasters-30211bf5428d), she puts numbers on the same routine: build a press list of 10 to 15 outlets and journalists, keep a communications calendar of hooks you can plan around (National Stuttering Awareness Week, International Stuttering Awareness Day, the anniversary of the ADA), and pitch in a why-what-how structure. Landing KQED, the Bay Area NPR affiliate, took her more than a year of follow-ups with different angles.

Someone heard that KQED segment, emailed her, and became a close friend; the interview went on to be nominated for an award. "You just never know who's listening to your story."

## Why was she good at PR for herself and not for clients?

Ten years of agency work and she'd never felt good at it. She was junior, pitching brands she didn't care about, never running the show.

"It's different when you're doing it for yourself and for a cause you really care about."

The skill had been there the whole time. What was missing was a reason to do the unglamorous part: the fifth follow-up, the hour spent reading a reporter's back catalog before writing four sentences. Caring is what pays for the hustle when results are months out. It's also the honest reason a lot of side projects die: the mechanics were fine, the topic wasn't something the founder wanted to think about on a Sunday.

Once the coverage reached the level she wanted, she scaled the PR push down deliberately and moved her attention to partnerships, events, and the film. Press was a phase.

## What happens when a side project outgrows one person?

It breaks you first, if you're built the way she is.

"I've always been the type of person that I do everything on my own," she said. She has structured her career to avoid managing people. Then: "I did get burnt out last year, and I just realized I need help."

The fix she chose was small and slow. Proud Stutter expanded beyond the podcast into events and programming and now operates as a sponsored project of Independent Arts & Media, a nonprofit arts service organization, which meant standing up a board. She kept it to three people plus herself, chosen from two and a half years of relationships, people she trusted not to steer the project somewhere she couldn't see. Her first board meeting had happened days before we spoke.

The money side has been a patchwork. California Humanities awarded her a $35,000 California Documentary Project grant in April 2023. She runs a gala. The one before our conversation beat its fundraising goal, with everything going to the documentary, and her plan was to keep running it annually until the film is fully funded.

## What can a clinician take from her therapy experience?

Chupkov's mother found a speech-language pathologist in Pasadena through the Stuttering Foundation's website. Maya didn't want to go. She went for her mom.

The therapy was fluency shaping and nothing else. "There was no talk of acceptance or goal setting."

Two things came out of that room. The first is what the SLP told her mother: that if she'd seen Maya earlier, she could have stopped her from stuttering. Her mother carried that as a private guilt for years: *why didn't I do this sooner*. The second is that Maya barely stuttered during sessions, which left the clinician puzzled about why she was there at all. She was very good at hiding it, and the clinical room was one more place to hide.

Set that beside the tip-of-the-iceberg model most of us were taught, the disfluencies above the waterline and the beliefs, avoidance and self-concept underneath, and the gap is obvious. She got the iceberg's tip worked on for months while the mass of it went unmentioned, in speech therapy and later in psychotherapy both.

I once worked with an eighteen-year-old who was socially confident, well-liked, and quietly avoiding reading aloud in class. What moved things for her was naming it out loud: *Hi, I'm Ashley, I stutter sometimes.* No technique came close. Chupkov's whole project is that sentence, scaled to a national audience. On variability she is blunt in a way the textbooks aren't: "Stuttering comes in many shapes and sizes, because every person deals with it in a different way."

## Where is Proud Stutter now?

Our conversation predates a lot. Since then, Chupkov's work has moved toward film and live convening. She is directing a feature documentary about Black men who stutter, examining how stuttering compounds with race and, in one subject's story, with incarceration and reentry; an earlier crowdfunding campaign for the project ran under the title *Rock of Hope*. In March 2026 Proud Stutter co-hosted a half-day gathering at the Arthur M. Blank Center for Stuttering Education and Research in Austin during SXSW, on stuttering and disability across film, media, education, and tech. In June 2026 she screened work-in-progress footage at the California State Capitol alongside Assembly Speaker Robert Rivas, with a reception raising toward a $20,000 goal for shoots in South Carolina.

Her day job is still a day job. She is the Media and Democracy Program Manager at California Common Cause, working on policy to sustain local and ethnic news outlets. Five years in, the side project has become the more visible half of her life without becoming the half that pays for it.

## What to take from this if you're building something on the side

Chupkov kept her job the whole way through, so this is no career transition and I won't dress it up as one. What she has is a repeatable method for building an audience around a narrow subject while employed full time, and four parts of it transfer cleanly to an SLP starting a podcast, a newsletter, a course, or a community.

Pick a slot and defend it, with permission to publish something small when the week goes badly. Pick a subject you'd think about anyway, because the unglamorous middle is long and interest is the only thing that reliably funds it. Treat press as a research problem: a list of named humans who already cover your topic, approached with a reason your story is different from the last one they wrote. And notice the moment when doing it all yourself stops being efficient. It will arrive. For her it arrived as burnout, with no memo in advance.

The thing she was hiding turned out to be the thing worth building on. That's not a metaphor for your caseload frustration, and I'd rather not force one. But if you've been sitting on a subject you know from the inside and haven't started because you can't picture how it gets sustained: the answer, at least in her case, is every other Friday, for years, around a full-time job.
