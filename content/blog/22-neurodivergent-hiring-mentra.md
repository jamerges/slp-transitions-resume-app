# How Hiring Screens People Out: Mentra's Founders on the Other Side of the Table

**The short answer:** Neither guest here is an SLP, and this isn't a transition story. Jhillika Kumar and Conner Reinhardt co-founded [Mentra](https://www.mentra.com), a hiring platform built for neurodivergent job seekers, and they spent the episode describing hiring from the employer's side of the table: what recruiters are actually paid to do, what a resume has no field for, and which accommodations cost a company nothing. If you're an SLP applying to non-clinical jobs, that's an unusually candid look at the machinery you're being fed into. If you counsel clients or families about work, Mentra is a resource to know about.

**Takeaways:**

- Why Reinhardt locates the barrier in the recruitment process, and what that means for anyone getting filtered out.
- The pitch that finally moved employers, and what it teaches you about framing your own value.
- Why the recruiter reading your application has different incentives than the leaders who approved the role.
- The ten accommodations people request most, most of which cost the employer nothing.
- Where the 2024 conversation is now out of date: Mentra's scale, its 2026 job-seeker survey, and its new AI career coach.

---

Kumar grew up seven years younger than her brother Vikram, who is autistic and nonspeaking. While she collected internships and degrees, he had no comparable access to any of it, and the gap sat on her as a question about how a society gets built. At Georgia Tech she was researching communication technology on a hunch about him: that the man everyone addressed like a three-year-old was, at 27, nothing of the kind. He got a letter board, then an iPad. "Letter by letter, he started to express himself," Kumar says, and what he expressed was an intelligence nobody had been able to see. He described the years before as being trapped in a body with no voice.

Reinhardt came at it sideways. He'd interned at Tesla in supply chain, cared about climate work, and had no particular exposure to neurodiversity when he organized the TEDx event where Kumar spoke about Vikram; he was her speaking coach for it. Her story landed on him as an engineering problem with a human center. "So much of his intelligence was actually misunderstood and therefore not really employed in the workforce," he says. He'd done well academically through school while getting bullied and struggling socially, and he received his own autism diagnosis at 24, after Mentra was underway.

They founded the company in Charlotte in 2021 with CTO Shea Belsky, raised a $1 million pre-seed from Sam Altman's Hydrazine Capital in early 2022 and a $3.5 million seed led by Shine Capital in 2023, and picked up a Microsoft AI for Accessibility grant along the way. Both founders are Forbes 30 Under 30 honorees. At the time of this recording the team was eleven people.

## What's actually broken in hiring, according to the people trying to fix it?

Reinhardt puts the problem in two directions at once, which is what makes it a business argument instead of a moral one. Companies hire people who then can't sustain the job. Companies also filter out people who could have done it well. Both are expensive, and both come out of the same screen.

"It has nothing to do with capabilities or skill set," he says of the population Mentra serves. "It has everything to do with the recruitment process itself."

## What finally convinced employers?

The pitch Mentra started with was the obvious one: here is a badly underemployed population. It didn't work. Kumar rewrote it around value: an untapped talent pool, quantified.

"You either want to show your customer how you're making the money or saving the money at the end of the day," she says, crediting the advice to someone else. In Mentra's case the saving is lower attrition and less brute-force screening, since the platform surfaces someone's cognitive profile and support needs before the interview instead of after the performance review.

The objection Reinhardt still fields is about effort. Employers want to know how hard this will be, and what they'll be on the hook to accommodate. He answers by pointing at their existing payroll. "Some of your best employees are neurodivergent already," he tells them. "You're already doing this."

Mentra learned the harder lesson downstream of the signed contract. Selling the leadership business case turned out to be a different job from getting a recruiter to use the product, because the two groups want different things. Recruiters, in Reinhardt's description, "just want to close roles and they want to find talent the hiring manager will accept as efficiently as possible."

So the rejection you read as a verdict on your fitness was, more often, a throughput decision. The person deciding whether your application advances is under pressure to hand their hiring manager a candidate who will get an easy yes. They are not adjudicating whether a speech-language pathologist could learn project management. Your application's real job is to be obviously easy to pass along: the title match visible in the first few lines, the relevant work near the top, no translation homework left for a stranger who is moving fast.

## What goes on a profile when it isn't a resume?

Mentra's design premise is universal design from the start, with no accommodation bolted on afterward. "We're building a better hiring process, period," Reinhardt says. "It just so happens that we're starting with the most complex use case."

What that looks like in practice is an onboarding flow Kumar says users tell her they've never encountered anywhere else: How does your brain work? What environments do you thrive in? What causes sensory overload? What manager support do you need, and what would you want teammates to know in week one? The output is a profile she calls the next generation of the resume, holding cognitive strengths, environmental sensitivities, communication preferences, projects, and work samples alongside the usual employment history. Roles concentrated first in data analytics, design and UX, and software engineering, after an early attempt to serve every job in every industry taught them to narrow. "Especially as an ADHDer," Kumar says of that impulse to chase everything at once.

Transparency runs the other way too. Candidates can see what a company actually offers: business resource groups, mentorship, whether the job description was written in a neuroinclusive way.

A resume has nowhere to put most of that. Not how you work, not the conditions that produce your best output, not the project you built that proves a skill none of your job titles imply. The format was designed for people continuing a straight line, which is why it squeezes career changers hardest.

## Which accommodations do people actually request?

Kumar rattled off Mentra's top ten, drawn from roughly 27,000 neurodivergent people on the platform at the time of the study: job coaching and mentoring, noise-cancelling headphones, extra time during interviews, flexible scheduling, uninterrupted work time, allowance of fidget devices, closed captioning and recorded materials, written concise instructions, an interviewer experienced with neurodiversity, and email or calendar organization support.

Most of them cost the employer nothing, because they are changes in manager behavior.

Her worked example is extra processing time. A candidate takes several seconds to formulate an answer; the recruiter reads the silence as a stall and quietly downgrades them. When the recruiter knows in advance, the pause stops registering as a problem. "I'm not going to disqualify them or create unintentional bias by the length of time they're taking to respond," Kumar says, voicing the recruiter. Later she adds another that costs nothing and changes a lot: don't make big decisions on the spot, because the loudest people in the room will otherwise steer them.

## Is telling an employer how you work a risk or a de-risking?

Companies ask Mentra whether knowing will bias them. Reinhardt sets medical diagnosis aside as its own separate conversation, then answers about the rest of it: strengths, communication style, what you need to do the work.

"There's more bias in not knowing that information," he says, "because if you don't know that information, then we'll be making judgments off of factors that are irrelevant to actually doing the job successfully."

He's also honest about when that disclosure currently has to happen. A stressful interview is the worst possible moment to attempt what he calls "this sort of double black diamond task of making your working with me document" and explaining it to a stranger. Mentra's whole design is an attempt to move that conversation earlier, when nobody's cortisol is up.

You can do a version of that unilaterally. Write your own working-with-me document on a calm Sunday: how you prefer to receive instructions, how you communicate under pressure, what your best working conditions are, what you need from a manager. Nobody has to see it. It exists so that when someone finally asks, you have something to read from.

## What's changed since this conversation?

The episode aired in March 2024, and Mentra has moved since.

Microsoft's own writeup of the partnership describes an AI matching engine weighing more than 76 data points and a platform with over 44,000 registered job seekers; Microsoft's AI for Accessibility team funded the work, and Azure partners helped migrate Mentra's machine-learning workloads. On placement quality, Reinhardt cited a 94.5% one-year retention rate on the episode, while Mentra's published materials and Microsoft's accessibility blog cite 97% retention for pre-vetted candidates and 79% of recommended candidates receiving interview invitations. Take the range for what it is: the same metric measured at different moments.

In March 2026 the company launched **Manatee**, an AI career companion offering daily job recommendations, resume and interview support, upskilling content, and nudges through a job search. Kumar's framing at launch: neurodivergent people are "about 15 to 20 percent of the total population" and still face significant barriers to employment.

Mentra's June 2026 survey of 265 neurodivergent professionals sharpened the picture. Nearly a quarter had been searching for work more than two years, against a national median unemployment duration under ten weeks. The most-cited barrier was lack of employer understanding, at 52.1%, with unclear job descriptions close behind at 49.1%. Once hired, 61.9% stayed with an employer three years or more, and 9.1% said they feel consistently valued. "The talent is here, they are highly skilled and remarkably loyal," Kumar said, "yet what's missing is a hiring system built to recognize it."

One number in the episode deserves a footnote. Both the intro and Reinhardt cite the claim that neurodiverse teams are around 30% more productive, sourced to Harvard Business Review's 2017 article "Neurodiversity as a Competitive Advantage" by Robert Austin and Gary Pisano. The article is paywalled and I couldn't confirm the exact figure inside it, so treat that one as a widely repeated claim and check it before you put it in a slide.

## What to take from this if you're job hunting outside the clinic

The screen is a process with its own incentives, and it is not a referendum on you. Recruiters are optimizing for speed and for a hiring manager's easy yes, so the fix is usually legibility, and rarely another credential.

The resume format undersells anyone changing direction, so put your evidence where the format can't hide it: a portfolio, a project write-up, a work sample the person reading can click.

Fit is a hard variable. Two thirds of the neurodivergent professionals in Mentra's survey had left a job or declined an offer over an environment that didn't work for them. SLPs leaving clinical work usually describe the same shape of problem: the caseload size, the documentation load, the conditions around the work. Ask about the conditions before you accept, since you now know which questions get answered honestly.

And if you work with clients or families around employment, Mentra is free for job seekers and open to anyone looking, including people who are currently employed and quietly considering what else is out there.

Kumar ended on evolution and access to new technology, and she put it plainly enough that it survives being pulled out of context: "We can't leave anyone behind. We just can't."
