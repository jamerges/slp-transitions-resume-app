# From Unpaid Hobby to a 50-Person Company: Meredith Harold on Building The Informed SLP

**The short answer:** Meredith Poore Harold finished a PhD at the University of Kansas, took a school SLP job to find out what the work actually felt like, stayed years longer than she planned to, and then spent nine months at home with infant twins between that job and a faculty position. During those nine months she started reading 60 to 80 journal articles a month and writing plain-language summaries for clinicians, free, as a hobby. A year later the email list had passed 10,000 people. Her husband pointed out that she was donating roughly 100 hours a month to it. She started charging, hired her first researchers out of the revenue, and [The Informed SLP](https://www.theinformedslp.com/) grew from there without outside money.

**Takeaways:**

- The question she asked herself before building anything, and why it beats "what's a good business idea."
- What a free year bought her that a business plan couldn't have.
- The mechanic that turned a hobby into a company: charging created hiring budget, and hiring created scope.
- How she staffed expertise she doesn't have, including the areas she'd openly fail a test on.
- The part she names as harder than any clinical job she's held.

---

Harold went straight from undergrad into a doctoral program and stayed at KU for a decade, working in research labs the whole time, the way other students take a part-time job. By the end of the program she wasn't sure she wanted academia, and in her words, she was getting a little burnt out. Family reasons meant staying in the same city, which breaks the standard academic script where you finish in one place and move for a faculty job somewhere else.

So she took a school-based SLP position, reasoning that if she was going to produce science for the field, she should know what it was like to actually do the job.

## What happens when a PhD walks into a school caseload?

She got flattened.

"I got my rear end basically handed to me. I had no idea how to be an SLP."

Harold describes herself at that point as a walking textbook who knew all the things and none of the work. Colleagues asked whether they should call her doctor. Her internal answer: probably not, since she had no idea what she was doing. The plan was a year, maybe two. Year one turned into year two, year two into year three, and it ran nearly six, because she loved it.

She left only because a local university came recruiting during a PhD shortage and talked her back into academia. Between the last school job and the first professor job sat nine months at home with twins.

## Where does a business idea come from when you weren't looking for one?

From a question, asked repeatedly, in the gaps of caring for two babies: "What can I contribute to our field that I'm uniquely qualified to offer and that would bring me joy?"

Her answer came out of the whiplash of those school years. Reading research for a clinical caseload and reading research for a dissertation are, as she puts it, completely different animals, and she didn't know that until she was the one with the caseload. A journal article runs 15 or 20 pages, and most of it is irrelevant to the clinician holding it. Somebody has to extract the usable part.

She was fast at reading research and knew exactly what school-based SLPs needed. So she committed to 60 journal articles a month, sometimes 80, and wrote up what clinicians needed to know. The project ran on one sentence: "This is what I would have wanted somebody to do for me."

Instead of surveying a market or hunting for whitespace, she found the one place where an unusual skill of hers overlapped a pain she had personally suffered, and then served the version of herself from three years earlier.

## Why give away 100 hours a month for free?

Because she wasn't building a company. "I started doing it as a hobby. Honestly, like I wasn't intending to start a business." Her comparison is a blogger who blogs because blogging is fun.

The free year did something a business plan couldn't. It proved demand at a scale nobody could argue with: an email list past 10,000 after twelve months, with open rates high enough that she knew people were reading rather than collecting. Then her husband, who works in business, made the obvious observation that she'd been donating about 100 hours a month and might consider charging.

"And I was like, okay, yeah, that makes sense."

Before you copy the move, look at the conditions. Behind her reading speed sat a decade of lab work, and behind the hundred monthly hours sat nine months without a job. A free year is never actually free. Something is paying for it.

## What actually turned it into a company?

Revenue bought labor. "Once I started charging people for it, I had money that I could hire on other people," she said. Researchers first, then writers. The business, in her word, bootstrapped from there, and the loop kept turning for a couple of years until the coverage went from school-age topics to the entire field, every niche in it.

At the time of this 2020 conversation, Harold put the staff at almost 60 people. Her framing of the problem they'd taken on: hundreds of journal articles publish in the field every single month, and expecting individual SLPs to dig through them alone is, in her words, appalling and hilarious and ridiculous.

Only about 5 to 10% of the field's research is immediately clinically applicable, meaning the kind where knowing the finding changes what you do this week. The rest is theoretical, or tests software that exists nowhere outside a lab, or images brains without yet telling anyone how to assess or treat differently. An SLP scanning titles and abstracts can't tell which is which until she has read the whole paper and lost the evening. That's the sorting Harold's team does, hundreds of times a month, so that nobody's Tuesday night disappears into a neuroimaging study she can't use.

## How do you hire for expertise you don't have?

Carefully, and in order. Harold hired closest to her own expertise first, expanding from school-age into early intervention, territory she knew well. Adult and medical topics waited until she was ready, because she isn't qualified to evaluate that work: "ask me a dysphagia question, I will fail it."

Her solution was to borrow judgment. When she hired the adult editor and the adult medical writers, she brought in PhDs on the back end to help her run the hiring. Everything published passes a minimum of three or four people, often six or seven.

The same sequence scales down to one person with a side project: stay in your lane until you can rent the judgment you're missing, then build a review process for every place your own gut isn't qualified to be the check.

## What does running it actually cost her?

"It's so much heavier than anything I've ever done before."

Asked whether she'd choose this path again, she said yes without hesitating. Asked how it feels, she was equally direct about the weight. Her response to that weight is structural: take as much off her own plate as she can, role release wherever the role can be released, and let a staff she describes as brilliant and thoughtful carry the decisions with her.

The heaviest part of the job is the controversial calls. Getting a harmful or pseudo-scientific practice out of clinical use has a name in science, de-implementation, and Harold says her company does the bare minimum of it, on purpose. Part of that is labor. Part of it is that publicly telling other businesses their courses and products shouldn't be used opens her company to lawsuits, a reality she describes as one she doesn't always love. And part of it is a line she holds hard: her team doesn't pick a side prematurely on live debates like the one over ABA. They scout the journals, publish what's clinically relevant, lay out the pros and cons, and let clinicians decide.

"The best we can do is be transparent. Be careful not to gatekeep information."

People text her constantly wanting a yes list and a no list. Refusing to be the field's endorser is, she says, the one place she has to censor herself.

## Where is The Informed SLP now?

Substantially past where this conversation left it. Public records list Harold as owner of The Informed SLP, LLC since 2015, and her CV shows an assistant professorship at Rockhurst University from 2016 to 2019. The company existed for years while she still held an academic job, and she left academia rather than launching from unemployment.

Today the site credits a team of 50+ SLPs and PhDs, houses 4,000+ clinical research reviews, and states that 40,000 SLPs use it each month, with individual membership at $12 a month billed annually and unlimited ASHA CEUs included. Team plans serve 450+ organizations. In December 2024 she launched a second business, [Informed Jobs](https://www.informedjobs.com/), an SLP jobs board with actual standards: every post reviewed by an SLP before it goes live, no ghost listings, and screens that reject roles paying below the 10th percentile or demanding productivity above 85%.

## What to take from this if you're still in the clinic

Four moves in Harold's path travel.

She built at the exact intersection of a skill almost nobody else had and a pain she'd personally suffered, rather than shopping for a market. She shipped it free long enough for demand to become undeniable, then let someone with business instincts tell her the obvious thing she was too close to see. She spent early revenue on people instead of on herself, which is what let a solo newsletter become a company covering an entire field. And she kept a paycheck while the thing grew.

The day-to-day work of running it is reading, synthesizing, translating jargon into plain language, hiring, editing, quality control, and holding an ethical line while people push on it. None of that is clinical practice, and all of it was built during clinical training. Harold says the scientists she covers are grateful that somebody is basically marketing their research for them.

That's a marketing job. An SLP is doing it, and she got it by inventing it.
