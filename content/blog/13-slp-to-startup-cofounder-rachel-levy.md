# SLP to Startup Co-Founder: Rachel Levy on Building the Bridge Herself

**The short answer:** After 16 years as a clinical SLP, Dr. Rachel Levy took a customer success role at a voice-technology startup, ended up doing sales, partnerships, research, and white papers under a single job title, then watched a colleague spin up his own company to resell the product. Her reaction was "if that guy can do it, then so can I." She lost sleep over how to pitch it, pitched it anyway, and co-founded The Babel Group with Katie Sieber — a consultancy connecting allied-health clinicians to the tech companies building products for them.

**Takeaways:**

- Why a startup job title covers roughly a tenth of what you actually do, and why that's the fastest skills education available to a clinician.
- How a mentor conversation — not an application — got her into tech.
- Why the questions you're embarrassed to ask are often the ones nobody in the room has thought to ask.
- The honest money answer: the business sustains itself and has not yet paid its founders.
- Where allied-health clinicians are already gathering to make this move, for free.

---

Rachel Levy had been a clinical SLP for about 16 years, mostly pediatrics, across schools and private practices, when she got restless. She went and got her doctorate, figuring it would springboard her somewhere new. She wasn't sure where. After the doctorate she opened a private practice, and knew fairly quickly that wasn't it either.

What actually moved her wasn't a credential. It was a conversation. She'd started networking more deliberately, and a mentor asked her a plain question: what do you want to be doing? Levy said she was interested in technology, and in articulation and accents. The mentor happened to be close friends with the founder of a voice-tech company called VoiceIt, made an introduction, and Levy was offered a customer success manager role.

She spent three years there.

## What does an SLP actually do at a voice-tech startup?

Officially, customer success. In practice, Levy describes the startup reality bluntly: you have a title, but the title covers about one tenth of what you'll actually be doing.

So the work included sales, business development, partnerships, research, pilot studies, and writing white papers. She sat in meetings with Intel. She was brought into pitches and customer-facing conversations — not to speak as a clinician, but to speak on a business level.

That took time. Her co-host on the episode, Mai Ling Chan, describes joining a brain-computer-interface company and not speaking during meetings for the first three months. Levy's experience was the inverse: she landed on a team that was, in her words, hanging on every word of her clinical expertise. She notes she didn't realize at the time how fortunate that was.

**Customer success** is the role that owns a customer *after* the sale — onboarding them, keeping them using the product, and keeping them from leaving. It shows up constantly in SLP transition stories because the underlying job is keeping people engaged in something over months, which is what a caseload already is.

## Why is "wearing ten hats" an advantage, not a warning sign?

Because it's compressed skills acquisition, and it's the part that transfers.

Levy went into VoiceIt with clinical expertise and came out with sales, partnership, and research-writing experience — none of which she would have accumulated in a role with clean boundaries. When the opportunity to start something appeared, she'd already done the constituent parts.

That's the pattern worth noticing if you're looking at a startup job and wondering whether the chaos is a red flag. Sometimes it is. But a job that forces you to do ten things badly for a year is also the fastest way to find out which of the ten you're good at.

## What made her leave a job she was good at?

A reseller stopped selling VoiceIt's product. Onboarding a new company to sell it takes a long time — you have to educate them on the system from the inside. Then one of VoiceIt's own salespeople said he had another company and could sell the product through it. VoiceIt said yes.

Levy's reaction: *"if that guy can do it, then so can I."*

She describes losing many nights of sleep working out how to present it. She didn't want to do it alone, so she brought in Katie Sieber, whom she'd known for years and who had worked at Project Euphonia before joining VoiceIt. Then she pitched a team of speech pathologists who would sell VoiceIt.

Privately, she and Sieber wanted it to be more than a one-product reseller. They wanted a bridge: companies building products get access to clinical consultants who help them develop and go to market in a way that actually serves the people the product is for.

## Are your "dumb questions" actually your value?

This is the most useful thing in the conversation for anyone about to walk into a room full of engineers.

Chan describes joining a brain-computer-interface company convinced she couldn't contribute, then slowly realizing her questions weren't dumb — they were exploratory, and they surfaced things the team hadn't considered because everyone there had been looking at the problem the same way for years.

Levy's framing of the trap is sharper: the more educated you get, the more you know what you don't know, and the more self-critical you become. She argues it should work the other way. The more you know, the more you can contribute what you already know.

That maps directly onto the thing that stops SLPs from applying to non-clinical roles at all — the assumption that not knowing the domain means having nothing to offer. Usually the opposite is true. You're the only person in the room who has met the end user.

## Is she making money?

Chan asked directly, which is rarer than it should be, and Levy answered without dancing.

The business is making money. That money sustains the business. It has not yet paid the founders anything.

She's clear-eyed about why that matters: she needs the business to make money to keep helping people, and the partners whose products she distributes are counting on her to make sales so they can keep developing. She says her husband calls her a dreamer. She agrees, and adds that she's a dreamer grounded in reality.

That's the honest shape of year one or two of a consultancy, and it's worth knowing before you romanticize the leap. "Sustaining itself" is a real milestone. It is not the same as a salary.

## Where do you find other clinicians doing this?

Sieber runs a free community called **Clinic to Code** that meets Fridays at noon. Each session brings on a professional who talks about their transition from clinical to non-clinical work. At the time of the conversation it had grown to roughly 200 people, and it's open to all allied-health professionals — SLPs, OTs, behavior therapists, nurses, educators.

Levy says the recurring questions are always the same three: how did you make the change, how did you find opportunities, how did you land them. And the recurring theme from the speakers is imposter syndrome — which she describes as real, and as something that stops people in their tracks.

If you're at the stage where you're reading exit stories at 11pm and haven't told anyone, a free weekly room full of people who already did it is a cheaper first step than any certificate.

## What this means for you

Three things in Levy's path generalize.

She got in through a **conversation, not an application**. The mentor introduction did what a cold résumé wouldn't.

She took a role that was **deliberately broad**, and the breadth became the qualification for what came next.

And she treated her clinical knowledge as **business input** rather than a separate credential to be set aside. The reason she got pulled into partnership meetings wasn't that she was a good employee. It was that she understood the user in a way nobody else on the team did.

One last note, because it reframes the whole conversation. Levy and Chan discuss the SLP shortage — usually delivered as doom. Levy flips it: opening more career paths may actually draw people *into* the profession, because you can tell someone they'll work face to face with clients *and* have somewhere to go afterward. A field with exits is a field more people are willing to enter.

Your degree isn't a prison. Sometimes it's the thing that gets you in the room.
