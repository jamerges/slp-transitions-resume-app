# SLP to Startup Co-Founder: Rachel Levy on Building the Bridge Herself

**The short answer:** After 16 years as a clinical SLP, Dr. Rachel Levy took a customer success role at [Voiceitt](https://www.voiceitt.com), a voice-technology startup, ended up doing sales, partnerships, research, and white papers under a single job title, then watched a colleague spin up his own company to resell the product. Her reaction: **"If that guy can do it, then so can I."** She lost sleep over how to pitch it, pitched it anyway, and co-founded [The Babel Group](https://www.thebabelgroup.com) with Katie Seaver, a consultancy connecting allied-health clinicians to the tech companies building products for them.

**Takeaways:**

- Why a startup job title covers roughly a tenth of what you actually do, and why that's the fastest skills education available to a clinician.
- How one mentor conversation got her into tech before she ever sent an application.
- Why the questions you're embarrassed to ask are often the ones nobody in the room has thought to ask.
- The money, plainly: the business sustains itself and has not yet paid its founders.
- Where allied-health clinicians are already gathering to make this move, for free.

---

Rachel Levy had been a clinical SLP for about 16 years, mostly pediatrics, across schools and private practices, when she got restless. She got her doctorate, hoping it would springboard her somewhere new. She opened a private practice afterward and knew fairly quickly that wasn't it either.

A conversation moved her. A mentor she'd started networking with asked a plain question: what do you want to be doing? Levy said technology, and articulation and accents specifically. The mentor happened to be close friends with Danny Weissberg, the founder of a voice-tech company called Voiceitt, and made the introduction. (Weissberg started Voiceitt in 2012 after a stroke left his grandmother's speech unintelligible to voice assistants and, often, to people around her. The company's automatic speech recognition is built specifically to parse atypical speech patterns.) Levy was offered a customer success manager role and spent three years there.

## What does an SLP actually do at a voice-tech startup?

Officially, customer success. Levy describes the startup reality bluntly: "You'll have a title, but the title will only cover one tenth of the things that you're actually gonna be doing." Sales, business development, partnerships, research, pilot studies, white papers: "technically I held like 10 titles." She sat in meetings with Intel. She was brought into pitches and customer-facing conversations to speak on a business level.

**Customer success** owns a customer after the sale: onboarding them, keeping them using the product, keeping them from leaving. It shows up constantly in SLP transition stories because the underlying job is keeping people engaged in something over months, which is what a caseload already is.

## Why is “wearing ten hats” an advantage?

Levy went into Voiceitt with clinical expertise and came out with sales, partnership, and research-writing experience she wouldn't have accumulated in a role with clean boundaries. When the opportunity to start something appeared, she'd already done the constituent parts. A job that forces you to do ten things badly for a year can also be the fastest way to find out which of the ten you're good at.

## What made her leave a job she was good at?

A reseller stopped selling Voiceitt's product. Onboarding a new company to sell it takes time; you have to educate them on the system from the inside. Then one of Voiceitt's own salespeople said he had another company and could sell the product through it. Voiceitt said yes.

"If that guy can do it, then so can I."

Levy describes losing many nights of sleep working out how to present it. She didn't want to do it alone, so she brought in Katie Seaver, whom she'd known for years and who had worked at Google's Project Euphonia before joining Voiceitt. Then she pitched a team of speech pathologists who would sell Voiceitt.

Privately, she and Seaver wanted more than a one-product reseller: "a bridge to innovation," in Levy's words, connecting companies building products with clinical consultants who help them develop and go to market in a way that actually serves the people the product is for. That bridge is now The Babel Group's public pitch: "Innovation with a conscience," clinical experts bringing assistive and therapeutic products into real-world practice. The current portfolio runs well past Voiceitt: automated report-writing software, AI-generated visual supports for therapists, feeding and swallowing tools, private speech-recognition microphones for the classroom.

Levy names the cost of building something nobody's tried before: "It's a challenge when you're trying to make headway and make change in a place that isn't currently ripe or ready for it, because they just don't know that it's possible." She calls the position "kind of lonely." It's a business built where "roads don't exist," a stretch she describes, half-joking, as heading out to the Wild West.

## Are your "dumb questions" actually your value?

Levy's framing of the trap: "The more educated you get, the more you know what you don't know, and the more self-critical you become." She argues it should run the other way: the more you know, the more you can contribute what you already know.

That maps onto the assumption that keeps SLPs from applying to non-clinical roles at all: that not knowing the domain means having nothing to offer. Usually the opposite is true. You're the only person in the room who has met the end user.

## Is she making money?

Asked directly, Levy didn't dance around it. The business makes money. That money sustains the business. It has not yet paid the founders anything.

"I have to say yes, because I have to believe in my mission." She's clear-eyed about the stakes: the partners whose products she distributes are counting on her to make sales so they can keep developing. Her husband calls her a dreamer. She agrees: "and even though I'm a dreamer, I am also grounded in reality."

That is what year one or two of a consultancy looks like, and you should know it before you romanticize the leap. A business that sustains itself has cleared a real bar. Paying its founders is the next one.

## Where do you find other clinicians doing this?

Seaver runs a free community called **Clinic to Code** that meets Fridays at noon. Each session brings on a professional talking through their own move from clinical to non-clinical work. At the time of this conversation it had grown to roughly 200 people, open to all allied-health professionals: SLPs, OTs, behavior therapists, nurses, educators.

The recurring questions, per Levy, are always the same three: how did you make the change, how did you find opportunities, how did you land them. The recurring theme underneath them is imposter syndrome: "real," and "something that stops us in our tracks."

## What this means for you

Three things in Levy's path generalize. A mentor's introduction got her in, and it did what a cold résumé wouldn't have. She took a role that was deliberately broad, and the breadth became the qualification for what came next. And she treated her clinical knowledge as business input. She got pulled into partnership meetings because she understood the user in a way nobody else on the team did.

Levy and her co-host discuss the SLP shortage on the episode, usually delivered as doom. Levy flips it: opening more career paths may draw people *into* the profession, because you can tell someone they'll work face to face with clients and have somewhere to go afterward. A field with exits is a field more people are willing to enter. ASHA seems to agree. It gave her and Seaver's talk, "Beyond the Bedside: Exploring Non-Clinical Career Pathways for SLPs," a Centennial honor badge at the 2025 convention, out of roughly 4,000 submissions.

Your degree got Levy into meetings with Intel. Sometimes it is exactly the thing that gets you in the room.
