# Speech Dude: How Chris Wenger Built an SLP Content Business Around a Full Caseload

**The short answer:** Chris Wenger, better known as [Speech Dude](https://www.speechdude.com/), was a full-time high school speech-language pathologist with a commute over 90 minutes each way and a growing family when he started posting short, funny, neurodiversity-affirming videos for other clinicians. At the time of this conversation he had roughly 250,000 followers on TikTok and 75,000 on Instagram, plus a speaking schedule that had just taken him to Australia. He was giving away the good stuff for free and describing the whole thing in terms of intrinsic motivation: **"I am intrinsically motivated to do the things I do because of the value and the meaning and the purpose behind it all."** Since then that free content has become a catalog — an assessment tool, courses, books, and a training academy he now runs with fellow SLP Jessie Ginsburg.

**Takeaways:**

- The market gap he built into, and why it was sitting in plain sight for every school SLP.
- Why he treats humor as a retention strategy rather than a personality trait.
- His reframe for online criticism, and the unglamorous tactic he pairs it with.
- The persuasion method he uses in IEP meetings that transfers directly to non-clinical work.
- What the free masterclass he mentioned on air turned into over the following four years.

---

Wenger recorded this episode in 2022, deep in a schedule most clinicians would call unworkable. Full-time caseload at a high school. Three-plus hours a day in the car. Speaking engagements. A social following in the hundreds of thousands. Asked to rank the hardest of them, he skipped all of it and named being a dad, which he called the most fun and the most time-consuming thing on the list.

## What gap was he actually building into?

Resources thin out as students get older. Elementary materials are everywhere; the SLP handed a caseload of fifteen-year-olds is improvising. Wenger says it plainly: "once they start getting old older, it becomes a little bit more challenging for the SLP to find materials."

Every school SLP has bumped into that wall. Wenger built his whole body of work into it, at the intersection of two underserved things at once: older autistic students, and the neurodiversity-affirming shift in how the field assesses and writes goals for them. The second gave the first its urgency. He frames the movement as a human rights movement rather than a methodology debate, and describes the model it replaces with some bite — teaching autistic kids "to pretend to be someone they're not," which he argues produces masking, a damaged self-concept, and eventually depression.

For anyone hunting a niche from inside the clinic, the question that finds one is which resource you spend an unreasonable amount of your own unpaid time building from scratch.

## Why does he treat humor as a strategy?

Because it does something measurable to memory, and he says so in almost instructional terms: "you can really take a concept and then add a bit of humor to it. So that way you're getting it to stick right, you tie emotion into something and that's how you make things stick in your memory."

The example he gives is a video about eye contact. Thirty seconds, a joke, and a clinician somewhere reconsiders a goal she has written a hundred times. He credits his dad for the cheesiness tolerance and refuses to dial it down for professional settings, including IEP meetings, on the grounds that nobody should ever meet him and think "hey, this isn't who I thought he was."

Clinicians already know the mechanism. Salience drives retention; that's the whole reason you use a preferred topic to teach a target. Wenger pointed it at an audience of adults.

## How do you survive being visible?

You get argued with, sometimes by strangers, sometimes about your right to speak at all. Wenger's frame: "Haters are love teachers, they provide value, because I will tell you that if you're starting to get haters, it means that you're starting to spread a word out there."

He extends it into the metaphor he clearly enjoys most, given that he was talking into a ring light at the time. "Light does attract angels, but light also attracts moths." His read is that the volume of pushback tracks with how far a message has traveled from the status quo: "They don't target things that are ordinary, they target things that are extraordinary."

Underneath the philosophy sits a far less lofty tactic. He deletes the comment, blocks the account, moves on, and sounds delighted about all three.

## What's the persuasion method?

Parents push back on neurodiversity-affirming goals. Some want their kid to blend in. Wenger doesn't argue, and he's explicit that arguing would fail: "People like to make their own discoveries, they don't like to be told what to do."

So he plants. His actual script in a meeting is to acknowledge where the parent is coming from, mention that he's been learning some new things, and ask permission to email a link or two afterward for them to look at on their own time. He calls himself a gardener, which on air became Chris Appleseed.

For anyone eyeing a non-clinical role, that skill has a market price. Customer education, clinical training, product marketing, sales engineering, implementation — every one of those jobs is the IEP meeting again, with a different person on the other side of the table who has already decided what they think. Clinicians who can move someone without triggering their defenses are doing the expensive part of those jobs before they've ever held the title.

## What kills a side project?

Distraction, and the specific kind that looks like productivity. "Don't get stuck being busy, being busy," Wenger says, arguing for staying "mono maniacally focused" on one thing, and admitting in the same breath that he still struggles with it.

His case study is the Netflix docuseries *Cheer*. The team wins in season one, then spends the gap between seasons collecting media attention and posting from vacations, and Wenger says he predicted the season-two result out loud before it aired, because they'd stopped doing the thing that made them win.

Then he names the thing that pulls people off course, and it's the one most business advice sells you. Money goals. "If I, you know, get my email list, and I sell this, I'm going to make a ton of money. Those are the things that will be distracting." The pull toward five courses and a product line arrives long before the audience does, and it fragments the work that would have earned one.

## What happens after you hit the goal?

Wenger's image for this is a lighthouse that keeps receding. You aim at it, and when you get close, it should have moved.

He illustrates it with Buzz Aldrin, who walked on the moon at 39 — second, after Armstrong — and then came home. Aldrin published a memoir in 1973 that broke the astronaut corps' silence about psychological difficulty, describing the depression and drinking that followed, and wrote about it again in 2009. Wenger's version of the diagnosis, in Aldrin's voice as he tells it: "Because I didn't have a goal after I met my goal."

For the SLP whose whole plan is *get out of the clinic*, that's a live risk rather than a parable. Exit is a destination, and destinations run out.

## What did the free masterclass become?

On air, Wenger's answer to "what's next" was a free masterclass on neurodiversity-affirming assessment, and a website. That was 2022.

Since then the free thing grew a paid ladder underneath it. He created the **Dynamic Assessment of Social Emotional Learning (DASEL)**, an informal, strengths-based assessment for autistic students covering self-advocacy, perspective-taking, problem-solving, sensory needs, and friendship priorities, sold as a digital kit with caregiver, teacher, and student forms. It sits alongside IEP and goal-writing courses, a neurodiversity-affirming activity book, a children's storybook, and an AI bootcamp for clinicians. He and Jessie Ginsburg — his partner, and the SLP behind [Sensory SLP](https://www.sensoryslp.com/) — now run the [NeuroAffirm Therapy Academy](https://www.sensoryslp.com/neuroaffirm-therapy-academy) and a weekly live show, and describe having trained tens of thousands of therapists between them, with a combined community they put at over a million followers across platforms. His [Instagram account](https://www.instagram.com/speechdude/) alone shows around 500,000 followers now, up from the 75,000 James congratulated him on during the interview.

As of a late-2025 profile of the two of them, Wenger was still described as a high school speech-language pathologist. He built the second career on top of the first and appears to have kept both, which makes his an expansion story rather than an exit.

## What to take from this if you're still in the clinic

Wenger's path has four moves in it that don't require a following.

He picked the gap he was already filling by hand. He gave away his best material until people trusted him enough to buy something. He built an energy system before he built a business, on the theory that if you start the day with something hard, "the rest of the day kind of takes care of itself." And he refused to let revenue goals set the direction, which is why the products, when they came, were things his audience had already told him they needed.

The first one is available to you this week. You know which resource you keep rebuilding from scratch on a Sunday night because nothing decent exists. Somebody else is rebuilding it too.
