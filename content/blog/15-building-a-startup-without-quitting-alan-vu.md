# Building a Startup Without Quitting Your SLP Job: Alan Vu on Flexspeak

**The short answer:** Alan Vu works full time as a speech-language pathologist at a school in Southern California, mostly with non-speaking students who use AAC. At the time of this conversation he was also running an e-commerce business, working as a marketing strategist for the National Stuttering Association, and building [Flexspeak](https://flexspeak.com), an AAC app that uses AI to generate a personalized vocabulary from the start. Four things at once. His own assessment: **"I would not recommend it."**

**Takeaways:**

- What Flexspeak actually does differently, and the abandonment statistic driving the product.
- What he delegates, and why an executive assistant was the unlock rather than working harder.
- Why he insists entrepreneurs are *calculated* risk-takers.
- How working in a school every day functions as his research pipeline.
- The moment the idea became viable, years after he first had it.

---

Vu started stuttering at four and began speech therapy in fourth grade, a period he remembers as genuinely fun: skipping class to play Kinect with his SLP, practicing strategies, working on desensitization.

By high school he'd play in a hardcore band and, by his own account, wasn't a great student. One elementary report card put it more bluntly: *"Alan is really disruptive, he keeps on making weird noises in class, and he keeps on disrupting other students."* He wouldn't be diagnosed with ADHD until college. Two days before orientation as a sociology major at Cal State Long Beach, he Googled jobs that make people the happiest, found speech-language pathologist near the top, thought about his own time in therapy, and switched.

Then something clicked. Once he was taking courses he cared about, he found he was a better student than he'd believed: "maybe I'm not as dumb as I thought I was."

## What is Flexspeak, and what gap is it filling?

**AAC**, augmentative and alternative communication, covers how non-speaking or minimally speaking people communicate: low-tech boards and books, or high-tech software and devices. Vu compares the term itself to blockchain or NFTs, technically precise and functionally intimidating, and often just says *assistive communication technology* instead.

The number underneath the product: **three out of five AAC systems are abandoned within the first year.** Existing systems, per the research Vu cites, are too complex, have a steep learning curve, and can be rigid.

Flexspeak's answer starts at setup. During onboarding, a caregiver shares what the user is interested in and what their daily routine looks like, and the app generates a personalized vocabulary from that data, compressing customization that normally takes weeks into the first session. Co-founded with Michael Jamieson and backed by Techstars, the company now ships two pieces: **Flexspeak AAC**, the communication app itself, and **Flexspeak Studio**, a tool for building visual supports fast, with content translated across 70-plus languages. That is the multilingual piece Vu was already building toward when he described wanting the system to "code-switch between the language spoken at school and the language spoken at home."

The name behind the mission is Bob Williams, an AAC user and policy director at Communication First, whom Vu quotes from memory:

> "If I could not express myself, I would become like the tree in the forest, the one for which it does not matter if it makes a sound when it comes crashing down, because there's no one around to hear it."

Flexspeak's tagline, *until every voice is heard*, comes directly out of that image.

## Can you actually build a company while working full time as a clinician?

Yes, and Vu wants you to know what it costs.

He was doing four things simultaneously when this conversation happened: the school job, Flexspeak, the National Stuttering Association marketing role, and Here & Now Supply, an e-commerce business he co-ran with his father. He wouldn't recommend it. Two moves made it survivable rather than unsustainable.

He prioritized ruthlessly and built through the margins of an already-full schedule. And he delegated the business that had matured enough to run without him: an executive assistant ran Here & Now day to day, freeing the bandwidth for Flexspeak and for being present with his students. (Here & Now Supply was acquired in 2023, after this conversation was recorded, so there is one less plate spinning today than when Vu described juggling four.)

## Why is the day job a research advantage?

Because he watches the failure happen in front of him.

Vu describes handing a device to a parent and seeing them interact with a complicated interface, unsure how it's even organized. That is his user research, and it happens on an ordinary Tuesday. Working at a site where he primarily serves non-speaking AAC students means the barriers are visible daily, and the people he's designing for are the people he already serves.

## What does "calculated risk taker" mean?

Vu pushes back on the idea that entrepreneurs are simply risk-takers. He calls them *calculated* risk-takers: identify a pain point users actually experience, validate it with real data where possible, then assess how saturated the market already is. AAC has a moderate number of competitors, which turns the question into how you'd do it meaningfully better, a belief he admits takes "a certain level of arrogance."

He's careful to add there's no one-size-fits-all AAC solution, and some people do well with existing systems. The gap he's aiming at is the majority who pick something up and can't sustain it.

## What made the idea viable?

He'd carried the intersection of technology and speech pathology for years without knowing what would make it stand out. ChatGPT's release changed that. Integrating AI stopped being speculative. He wasn't confident existing providers would move quickly, so he decided he might as well be the one to do it.

He didn't force the idea during the years it wasn't ready. He kept it, and moved once the enabling technology arrived.

## Why he cares about connecting users to each other

Vu ties this back to his own experience with the isolation of a communication disability, and to finding a stuttering support group where people could talk openly about their experiences.

A core Flexspeak goal is letting AAC users connect with each other rather than being stuck on a single platform: messaging a friend who uses a different app, texting a parent that you miss them or want a cheeseburger for dinner. He wants the industry collaborative enough to make systems interoperable, and says explicitly he doesn't see it as winner-take-all. It's a pointed observation about a field built entirely around communication: the companies in it ought to be able to talk to each other too.

## What to take from this if you're still in the clinic

Vu kept the job that gives him users and income. He delegated the business that had matured enough to run without him. He waited for the technology that made his idea distinct instead of forcing it early. And he chose a problem he lives inside every working day.

If you have an idea you're not acting on, ask which of those four moves you could make this month.
