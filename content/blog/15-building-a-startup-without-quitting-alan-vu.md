# Building a Startup Without Quitting Your SLP Job: Alan Vu on Flex Speak

**The short answer:** Alan Vu works full time as a speech-language pathologist at a special education school, mostly with non-speaking students who use AAC. He also runs an e-commerce business he started in grad school, works as a marketing strategist for the National Stuttering Association, and is building Flex Speak — an AAC app that uses AI to generate a personalized vocabulary from the start. Four things at once. His own assessment of that: **"I would not recommend it."**

**Takeaways:**

- What he delegates, and why an executive assistant was the unlock rather than working harder.
- The statistic driving the whole product: three out of five AAC systems are abandoned within the first year.
- Why he calls entrepreneurs *calculated* risk-takers, not risk-takers.
- How working in a school every day is his research advantage, not a distraction from the startup.
- The moment the idea became viable — and it wasn't when he had the idea.

---

Vu started stuttering at four and began speech therapy in fourth grade. He remembers it as genuinely fun: skipping class to play Kinect with his SLP, practicing strategies, working on desensitization. Empowering, in his word.

By high school he was, by his own description, not a great student — disruptive, skipping class, more interested in the hardcore band he played in than school. He was later diagnosed with ADHD in college. Two days before his orientation as a sociology major, he Googled jobs that make people the happiest, found speech-language pathologist near the top, thought about his own time in therapy, and switched.

Then something clicked. Once he was taking courses he cared about, he found he was a much better student than he'd believed — and, in his words, maybe not as dumb as he'd thought.

## Can you actually build a company while working full time as a clinician?

Yes, and he wants you to know it costs something.

Vu is doing four things simultaneously and says plainly that he wouldn't recommend it. What makes it survivable isn't stamina — it's two specific moves.

**He prioritizes ruthlessly.** He built the e-commerce business through grad school, which taught him what fits in the margins of an already-full schedule.

**He delegates the thing that runs itself.** He has an executive assistant running the e-commerce business day to day. That's what frees the bandwidth for Flex Speak and for being a good SLP to his students.

That ordering matters. The instinct when you're overloaded is to work more hours. His answer was to hand off the mature business so the new one and the day job could both get real attention.

## What problem is Flex Speak solving?

**AAC** — augmentative and alternative communication — is the umbrella term for how non-speaking or minimally speaking people communicate: low-tech boards and books, or high-tech software and devices. Vu calls the term itself intimidating, and compares it to how people talk about blockchain or NFTs. He sometimes just says *assistive communication technology* instead.

The number underneath the product is this: **three out of five AAC systems are abandoned within the first year.** Existing systems, per the research he cites, are too complex, have a steep learning curve, and can be rigid.

Flex Speak's approach is to use actual user data at setup. During onboarding you share what the user is interested in and what their daily routine looks like, and the app generates a personalized vocabulary from that — so the customization that normally takes a long time is largely done before anyone starts.

The longer goal is a context-aware system: one that takes in where the user is, who they're talking to, and can code-switch between the language spoken at school and the language spoken at home. He's explicit that current features are foundational and the ambition is to have it feel like an extension of the user's voice.

## Why is the day job an advantage rather than a distraction?

Because he watches the failure happen in front of him.

Vu describes handing a device to a parent and seeing them interact with a complicated interface, unsure how it's even organized. That's not a user-research session he had to commission. It's Tuesday.

Working at a site where he primarily serves non-speaking students who use AAC means the barriers are visible daily, and the people he's designing for are the people he already serves. For any clinician wondering whether to quit before building something — that access is worth more than the extra hours quitting would buy.

## What does "calculated risk taker" mean?

Vu pushes back on the idea that entrepreneurs are risk-takers. He calls them *calculated* risk-takers.

His process: identify a pain point users actually experience, ideally validate it with real data rather than a hunch, then assess how saturated the market is. AAC has a moderate number of competitors, which means the question becomes how you'd do it meaningfully better — and he acknowledges that takes a certain level of arrogance to believe.

But he's careful to say there is no one-size-fits-all AAC solution, and that some people do genuinely well with existing systems. The gap he's aiming at is the majority who pick something up and can't sustain it.

## What made the idea viable?

Not having the idea. He'd had the intersection of technology and speech pathology in mind for a while and wasn't sure what would make it stand out.

What changed was ChatGPT's release — at which point integrating AI stopped being speculative. He also wasn't confident existing providers would move quickly, and figured he might as well be the person who did.

There's a useful lesson in that gap between *having an idea* and *the idea becoming buildable*. He didn't force it during the years it wasn't ready. He kept it, and moved when the enabling technology arrived.

## Why he cares about connecting users to each other

This is the part he circled back to unprompted.

Vu ties it to his own experience with the stigma of a communication disability — which he describes as isolating — and finding a stuttering support group where people could talk about their experiences. Community, built through vulnerability and self-expression.

So a core goal for Flex Speak is letting AAC users connect with each other rather than being stuck on a single platform: messaging a friend who uses a different app, texting a parent that you miss them or that you want a cheeseburger for dinner. He'd like the industry to be collaborative enough to make systems interoperable, and is clear he doesn't see it as winner-take-all.

Which is a slightly pointed observation about a field built entirely around communication: it would be strange if the companies in it couldn't talk to each other.

## What to take from this if you're still in the clinic

Vu's path isn't "quit and go build." It's narrower and more repeatable than that.

He kept the job that gives him users and income. He delegated the business that had matured enough to run without him. He waited for the technology that made his idea distinct instead of forcing it early. And he chose a problem he lives inside every working day.

If you have an idea you're not acting on, the useful question isn't whether you're ready to leap. It's which of those four moves you could make this month.
