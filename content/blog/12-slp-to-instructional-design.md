# SLP to Instructional Design: Portfolio Over Certificates

Let me save you $3,000 in the first paragraph: **you do not need a certificate to become an instructional designer.**

You don't need a second master's either. I know — when you're an SLP, the reflex is strong. Our entire field runs on credentials. Degree, clinical fellowship, license, continuing ed, more continuing ed. Somewhere along the way, "get certified" became our default answer to every career question, the way I default to adding one more topping at Yogurtland even though I know — I *know* — it stopped improving around topping four.

But instructional design doesn't work like our field. **It's a portfolio-first field: no degree or certificate required.** Hiring managers don't hire the credential; they hire the work samples. Which is genuinely good news for a career changer, because a portfolio is something you can build on your own timeline, on your own couch, for close to free.

So let's talk about what actually opens the door.

## What does an instructional designer actually do?

Short version: instructional designers build learning experiences — corporate training courses, onboarding programs, e-learning modules, the stuff that teaches adults how to do things. You take messy subject-matter knowledge and turn it into a sequence that changes what someone can do.

If that sentence gave you a small jolt of recognition, it should. You've spent your career taking something impossibly complex — language, swallowing, cognition — and breaking it into teachable steps, sequenced from where the learner is to where they need to be, with data to prove it worked.

You've been designing instruction the whole time. You just called it therapy.

## Do you really not need a degree or certificate?

Really. **No degree or certificate is required** to get hired in instructional design.

This is the part that feels illegal to SLPs, so let me say it a third time in different words: there is no license to practice instructional design. There's no board. Nobody checks. What hiring teams evaluate is the portfolio — can this person actually design a course? — and no certificate answers that question, while three good work samples do.

Does that mean certificates are worthless? Not exactly — a structured program can give you deadlines and feedback if you're the kind of person who needs external accountability (hello, fellow deadline-motivated clinicians). But it's a *convenience purchase*, not a requirement, and the expensive ones are selling you confidence more than access. The career-change market is full of expensive, generic help — one transitioner I've studied burned through a $220 resume writer, a $480 career coach, and a whole transition program before finding what actually worked. Don't repeat that arc with a $3k ID certificate when the door was never locked.

If you have $3,000, the portfolio route needs almost none of it. Spend the difference on your loans. Or, fine, your cat's grain-free pate budget.

## What actually gets you hired?

**A portfolio of 3-5 samples built in Articulate Storyline 360 or Rise, each with a process document.**

That's the whole gate. Let me break it down:

**The tools.** Articulate Storyline 360 and Rise are the industry-standard authoring tools — Storyline for custom interactive modules, Rise for clean, responsive courses. Articulate offers a free trial, which is enough runway to build real samples if you plan before you open the software (storyboard first, click around second — advice I give you from experience, having done it in the wrong order).

**The samples.** 3-5 finished pieces. Not twenty. Quality and range beat volume: maybe one interactive scenario, one Rise course, one job aid or micro-learning piece. And here's the detail career changers always miss —

**The process doc.** Each sample needs a short write-up of *how* you built it: the audience, the learning objectives, the design decisions, what you'd measure. This is what separates "person who learned Storyline" from "designer who thinks in outcomes." It's also where your clinical brain gets to show off, because writing objectives and measuring outcomes is your native language.

**And yes — spec work is fully acceptable.** You don't need a client or an employer to have a portfolio. Invent a realistic project: "onboarding module for new home-health clinicians," "hand-hygiene refresher for a hospital unit." In instructional design, made-up-but-realistic projects are a normal, respected way to demonstrate skill. Nobody will ask why a company didn't pay you for it.

Notice what a portfolio also does for you strategically: it's *proof of investment in the new field* — which happens to be one of the hidden reasons career changers get rejected. A hiring manager looking at five finished Storyline pieces isn't wondering whether you're serious.

## What vocabulary gates the interviews?

Here's the catch nobody tells you: the portfolio gets you the interview, but the *vocabulary* gets you through it. Instructional design has a canon, and interviewers use it as a shibboleth. The good news: you already know most of these concepts under different names.

**ADDIE** — Analyze, Design, Develop, Implement, Evaluate. The field's classic process model. You already run this loop: assess, plan, treat, measure, adjust. It's the therapy process wearing a lanyard.

**Kirkpatrick levels** — the four-level model for evaluating whether training worked: did they like it, did they learn it, did behavior change, did results follow. You know this one in your bones. **Progress monitoring is Kirkpatrick evaluation** — you never once assumed a session "worked" because the client enjoyed it; you took data on the behavior change.

**Mayer's multimedia principles** — research-based rules for how people learn from words and pictures (don't read slides aloud, cut the decorative clutter, segment content). If you've ever built a visual support or simplified a home program because the family glazed over, you've been applying these informally.

**Andragogy** — how adults learn, as opposed to children: they're self-directed, they bring experience, they need relevance. Anyone who's coached parents or trained nursing staff has done andragogy under fluorescent lights.

And the direct skill mappings, for your resume and your interview stories:

- **Therapy goal writing → learning objectives.** You've written measurable, criterion-referenced objectives for years. ID teams argue about how to do this at meetings. You'll be the calm one.
- **Session sequencing → curriculum scaffolding.** Ordering skills from foundation to mastery, adjusting the ramp when the learner struggles — that's scaffolding, and cueing hierarchies are a masterclass in it.
- **Progress monitoring → Kirkpatrick evaluation.** Baseline, data, decision. Most corporate training never gets past "did they like it." You'll arrive already thinking in outcomes.

Learn the terms, then practice saying your clinical stories *in* them. "I wrote measurable objectives, sequenced instruction to reach them, and monitored outcome data" is an instructional design sentence. It's also just… your Tuesday.

## How long does the transition really take, honestly?

**6-12 months is the typical timeline.** That's real-world, not brochure — time to learn the tools, build 3-5 portfolio pieces with process docs, absorb the vocabulary, and run a proper search.

And I'd be doing you a disservice if I skipped this part: **this market has gotten more competitive, largely because of the wave of teachers leaving education.** Teachers discovered instructional design a few years before SLPs did, and the entry-level pool is deeper than it used to be. I'm not telling you this to scare you off — I'm telling you because "either too vague or too discouraging" is the failure mode of career advice, and you deserve the middle: it's a real path with real competition, which means the differentiators matter more.

Your differentiators, specifically: healthcare fluency (health systems and health-tech companies need designers who already speak clinical), genuine outcome-measurement experience (rarer in the applicant pool than you'd think), and adult-teaching reps with the least motivated learners imaginable. A teacher taught students who had to be there. You taught a grandfather his swallow strategies while he told you they were nonsense. That's andragogy on hard mode.

General job-search physics still apply: referrals decide most successful transitions, generic mass-applying is what fails, and postings mostly get seen in their first day or two. Budget your energy accordingly.

## What should your first month look like?

If you want a concrete on-ramp instead of a vibe:

1. **Weeks 1-2:** Start the Articulate trial. Storyboard your first spec project on paper first — pick a healthcare topic you could teach in your sleep.
2. **Weeks 2-4:** Build piece #1 in Rise (the gentler tool), write its process doc, and start a running glossary of ADDIE, Kirkpatrick, Mayer, and andragogy with your own clinical examples next to each term.
3. **Ongoing:** One portfolio piece every few weeks until you have 3-5. Meanwhile, translate your resume — goals become learning objectives, sessions become scaffolded instruction, data collection becomes evaluation.

## So is instructional design right for you?

Here's my honest filter. This path fits if the part of SLP work you'd rescue from the burning building is *designing the learning* — writing the goals, sequencing the plan, watching the data curve bend — and what you'd happily leave behind is the caseload math and the paperwork treadmill.

It fits less well if you're mostly drawn to "remote job that isn't therapy," because the 6-12 month build and the competitive market will test a lukewarm why.

But if you read the ADDIE section nodding — if some part of you thought *wait, I already do this* — that's worth paying attention to. Your degree isn't a prison. The skills compound. You've been an instructional designer without the title for years; the portfolio is just the paperwork.

And for once, it's paperwork that works for you.
