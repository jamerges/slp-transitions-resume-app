If you're reading this at 11pm with a treatment room's worth of unfinished notes waiting for you, hi. I've been you.

I want to say the thing nobody said to me when I was googling variations of this exact question: wanting out doesn't make you a bad clinician. It doesn't mean you wasted your degree. It doesn't even necessarily mean you should quit.

It means you need a real way to decide, instead of spinning in the same 11pm loop for another year.

Most of what you'll find on this question is either too vague ("explore your options!") or too discouraging ("you'll have to start over"). Neither is useful at 11pm. So here's the decision process I wish I'd had.

## Is it burnout, or is it you?

Your brain is probably whispering that everyone else seems to handle it fine. Start there.

I genuinely enjoyed connecting 1-1 with students and clients. What sent me looking at career paths outside of SLP was the red tape, the never-ending paperwork, and the uncontrollable caseloads. Every item on that list is a *condition* around the work, and the kids never made the list.

There were days I felt like a customer service representative. One with three degrees.

Burnout in this field is mostly structural. You can't productivity-hack your way out of a caseload you don't control. You can't self-care your way out of documentation requirements that eat your evenings. When the system is designed in a way that grinds people down, the people getting ground down are not the problem.

**If you believe your exhaustion is a personal failure, you'll try to fix yourself. If you understand it's structural, you can ask the better question: do I want to keep working inside this structure?**

That's a real question with more than one honest answer.

## Are you in a bad workplace, a bad fit, or a bad season?

Almost nobody separates these out, which is why so many SLPs either quit too fast or stay stuck too long. "I want to quit" is three different problems wearing the same trench coat.

**A bad workplace** means the profession might be fine and your setting is not. The thing draining you is a specific administrator, a specific building, a specific productivity requirement. That's a job problem. And I have to be honest with you, even though it's less dramatic: sometimes the answer is a different setting. Schools to private practice. SNF to outpatient. Full-time to part-time. If the *clinical work itself* still lights you up when the conditions are decent, change the conditions first. It's faster, and it protects you from trading one burnout for a new-field version of the same burnout.

**A bad fit** means the conditions could be perfect and you'd still feel it. The sessions themselves drain you. You catch yourself more interested in the data, the training, the coordination, and the technology around the therapy than in the therapy itself. You did the setting-switch already and the feeling followed you. That's your interests telling you something, and they usually don't stop.

**A bad season** means life outside work is taking everything you've got: new baby, health stuff, grief, a move. *Any* job would feel impossible right now. This one deserves real caution, because burnt-out brains genuinely struggle with executive function, and planning a career change is one of the most executive-function-heavy things you can do. If you're in a bad season, survive it first. Reduce hours if you can. Lower the bar. Make the big decision when you have a brain that can make it.

Three problems. Three different answers. The 11pm loop happens when you try to solve all three with one giant terrifying question: *should I quit?*

Ask the smaller question instead: *which one is this?*

## What psychology traps keep SLPs stuck?

Even once you know which problem you have, your brain will fight the answer, because it thinks it's protecting you. Sunk cost makes the tuition feel refundable if you just stay long enough. Loss aversion makes the license you'd leave feel heavier than anything you'd gain. And identity fusion turns "I work as an SLP" into "I *am* an SLP," so a job change starts to feel like a small death.

I've written about those traps at length in [5 hidden fears stopping SLPs from making a career change](https://slptransitions.com/5-hidden-fears-stopping-slps-from-making-a-career-change/), including the reframes that actually loosened them for me. If you recognise yourself in that paragraph, read that one next.

For the decision in front of you right now: the degree is spent either way. The tuition is gone whether you stay or go. Staying miserable doesn't refund it. The only live question is what the next ten years look like.

One thing that post doesn't cover, because it belongs to the decision rather than the fear: some corners of our field treat wanting out as abandoning the kids, and you may get that reaction when you say it out loud. A field that needs its clinicians to be miserable to prove devotion hasn't earned it. The clients who come after you deserve someone who wants to be in the room.

## What does leaving actually look like, honestly?

Okay. Say you've worked through the above and you're landing on "bad fit." What are you actually signing up for?

Not a fantasy and not a catastrophe. Just the math.

**The timeline:** most successful SLP transitions take **6–15 months**. Some take 1.5–2.5 years, especially the technical paths. Nothing about this is a weekend decision that changes your life by fall. You run it as a project alongside your job, on purpose, while your income keeps coming in.

**Where SLPs actually land**, ranked by how often it really happens:

- **Project/program management**. The most-traveled road, with documented six-figure outcomes.
- **Healthcare data analysis**. Real people making $20k more than they did as SLPs. The entry point is healthcare orgs rather than big tech.
- **Customer success and implementation at health-tech and edtech companies**. The best effort-to-odds ratio, roughly $75–120k, and at speech-tech companies your CCC-SLP is literally the credential.
- **Marketing and content**. $80k–141k for the folks who've made it work.
- **Fast clinical-adjacent exits**: clinical/rehab liaison ($84k–135k) and utilization review ($80–88k), where your clinical background is the qualification.

Almost every path on that list *keeps the part of the work you probably loved*. Explaining complex things to stressed-out humans. Coordinating messy stakeholders. Reading data and turning it into decisions. Building trust with people who are skeptical. You don't abandon those skills when you leave. You stop doing them under conditions that were burning you down.

One warning from someone who's watched a lot of these transitions: don't expect to start a new career path and instantly have a six-figure salary with autonomy, flexibility, impact, creativity, and meaning all at once. First jobs out are bridge jobs. They trade some things to get you others. That's fine. That's how it works.

## How do you actually decide in the next 30 days?

A small, finite experiment beats another year of 11pm googling. Thirty days, three moves.

**1. Book three informational interviews.** Find SLPs who've moved into paths that interest you and ask for 20 minutes. LinkedIn is full of us. Expect some silence: around 20–25% of these asks get no response, which is normal and says nothing about you. The ones who *do* respond will give you something no blog post can: what the job actually feels like on a Tuesday. Ask what they miss. Ask what they don't.

**2. Make one small proof artifact.** One tiny piece of evidence in a direction you're curious about, and skip the certification or the course for now. Rewrite three of your resume bullets in non-clinical language. Pull the outcome data from your own caseload and chart it. Draft a one-page training doc like the ones you already make for parents, but aimed at a product. Two hours, max. You're testing how making it *feels*, more than the artifact itself. Energizing is data. Draining is also data.

**3. Take the one-minute career quiz.** I built a [short quiz](https://app.slptransitions.com/quiz) that matches your working style and interests to the non-clinical paths where SLPs actually land. It takes a minute. It won't decide for you. Nothing should. But it's a decent compass for which informational interviews to book first.

At the end of 30 days you won't have quit anything. You'll have information instead of anxiety. And in my experience, that's when the decision stops feeling like a cliff and starts feeling like a door.

Maybe you'll walk through it. Maybe you'll change settings instead. Maybe you'll realize it's a season and give yourself permission to just get through it.

All three are wins. The only losing move is another year of the loop.
