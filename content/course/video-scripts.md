# Transition OS: video scripts

One script per lesson (37), plus the video for the pre-order page. Each runs 60 to 100 seconds at a normal speaking pace. Every fact in them is already in the lesson it belongs to, and the only personal details are ones James has confirmed. A line in brackets marked YOUR STORY is a place where a real memory of yours would help: say it in your own words or skip it. Nothing in brackets should be read aloud.

## Before you film

- **Order.** Film batch 1 first. It is the pre-order video, the welcome, and the first lesson of each module: nine videos, about twelve minutes of footage, one afternoon. Batch 2 is everything else and can be filmed a module at a time.
- **Length.** If a take runs past two minutes, cut a paragraph instead of talking faster. The page under the video carries the detail.
- **Framing.** Eye level, a window in front of you, the same spot each time so the videos match. Look at the lens, and read a paragraph, look up, say it.
- **One point per video.** Each script ends by telling the viewer the one thing to do on the page. Keep that line even if you rewrite the rest.
- **Your words.** These are written to sound like you, and you will say them better than they read. Change anything that you would not say out loud. Do not add numbers that are not in the lesson.
- **When a video is uploaded,** add one line to `lib/course-videos.ts` with the lesson id and the link. The lesson then opens with the video and its short version.

## 0.3 Bad workplace, bad fit, or bad season?

"I want to leave" can mean three different things, and they have three different fixes.

It might be a bad workplace. The caseload is impossible, the productivity target is absurd, your supervisor is the problem. The work itself is fine. A different building might fix it.

## 1.1 You're allowed to want out (batch 1)

Welcome to Module 1. By the end of this week you'll have your reasons for leaving in writing, which sounds small and is the thing most people skip.

Most people leave in five stages, and each stage has its own false belief.

First is private doubt. You haven't told anyone, and you believe that looking means deciding. It doesn't. Looking is research.

Second is guilt. You believe leaving wastes the degree. The degree goes with you.

Third is permission. You believe it works for other people. The people whose stories are on the site started where you are.

## 2.5 Epic, MSL and UX research: what they really require

Three pieces of advice cost SLPs real money, and you'll hear all three.

One: "Get Epic certified." You can't buy it. Epic certification comes through an employer that sponsors you. The real ways in are junior analyst roles that sponsor, go-live support gigs, and consulting firms. The credential you can buy yourself is called CAHIMS.

Two: "Become a medical science liaison." That job is gated behind a doctorate. The cousin you can actually get is clinical liaison, which pays $84,000 to $135,000.

Three: "Go into UX research." The field grew about 35 percent more graduates in five years while openings stayed flat. The realistic route is healthtech, with two to four deep case studies.

Each of these myths has a product attached. Somebody is selling the shortcut, which is why the myth keeps going around.

# Module 3: Connect

## 3.1 Where to find people in your target field (batch 1)

Welcome to Module 3. This is the one that actually gets people hired, and it's the one SLPs skip.

Here's the problem. Your network is almost entirely SLPs. That's why nobody in it can refer you.

The people who hold your target job are in three places. The subreddit for that job title, where the complaint threads tell you what the work is really like. The professional community that serves it. And the companies on your list.

When I was looking, I joined Slack and Facebook groups for UX and content strategy, the two fields I was considering. Then I looked for SLPs in those groups who had already made the move, and I messaged them, because a former SLP's path was closer to mine than anyone else's.

You can do the same thing on LinkedIn in five minutes. Search a company on your list, add "CCC-SLP," and you'll find people who made your exact move.

And look at your own cart. The companies whose products you already use with clients are your first list. "I've used your product with clients" is an opener nobody else has.

Save three names before you write a single message.

## 3.2 Messages that get answered

The message that gets answered is short, it's specific, and it asks for fifteen minutes about their path with no job attached.

Let me tell you what got me hired. I saw that the company I work for now had just raised a funding round. I wrote to one of their recruiters to say congratulations. I didn't ask for anything. It started the conversation that became my job.

So the first rule: don't ask for a job, a referral, or a résumé review in the first message.

Name one specific thing about them. The move they made, a post they wrote. Ask one question they could answer in a sentence. And make a yes cheap: offer two time windows, and a one-question fallback if a call is too much.

About one in four of these never gets a reply. That's a volume problem. It isn't a verdict on you.

Follow up once, seven to ten days later. Then stop.

And at the end of every conversation, ask: "Who else should I be talking to?"

# Module 4: Translate

## 4.1 How your résumé gets screened, and why AI-written ones get rejected (batch 1)

Welcome to Module 4. You're going to rewrite your résumé so a hiring manager can see what you already do.

First, how it gets read. A recruiter spends about seven seconds deciding whether you're a maybe or a no. Those seconds go to the top third of page one.

You've probably heard that software rejects 75 percent of résumés. That number came from a sales pitch in 2012. The systems rank, and humans reject. The real automatic filter is the knockout questions on the application form.

Four doubts reject career changers. Will they go back to clinical work? Can I map this clinical language to my job? Have they invested in the switch? And do they want more money than this pays?

Now the newer problem. One hiring manager found 35 percent of applicants had sent identical AI-written answers, and 62 percent of employers say they reject AI materials that aren't personal.

So use the tool to interview you. Don't let it write your sentences.

Try the seven-second test below with someone outside healthcare. Then write three details only you could put on the page.

## 4.2 Translating clinical work into business language

Translation means describing work you already did in words the reader already knows.

Here's what goes wrong. People swap the nouns. "Patients" becomes "clients," "treatment plan" becomes "project," and the sentence still describes a duty. That's how expensive résumé rewrites turn into word salad.

A bullet that works answers three questions in one sentence. How big was it? What did you do? What changed?

So "provided therapy to a caseload of students" becomes a sentence with the number of students, the thing you built or ran, and what was different afterward.

Two rules. Pull the verb from the job posting, and leave your number where it already is.

And an honesty test. Could you talk for ten minutes about the day behind that bullet without making anything up? If not, rewrite it until you could.

One more. Keep "Speech-Language Pathologist" as your title. Put the translation in a line underneath it. Never rename the job.

Take one of your own bullets and rebuild it in the tool below.

## 4.3 Numbers you already have

No number, no bullet. That's the rule for this whole module.

You have more numbers than you think. Most of them were never written down, because nobody has ever asked an SLP "by how much?"

There are three kinds. Size: your caseload, the buildings you covered, the evaluations you did in a year. Rate: how many a day, how fast. Those two are already sitting in your calendar.

The third kind is change, and you have to build it. It comes from something you quietly fixed. The schedule you rebuilt. The screening that started getting done on time. What was the number before, and what was it after?

Estimates count if you can defend them. If you're not sure, round down, and put brackets around anything you still need to check.

Rebuild at least one number from the source, not from memory. In an interview, someone will ask where it came from.

## 4.6 Writing a cover letter worth reading

A cover letter gets read as evidence of one thing: that you spent effort on this company and no other.

That's why a generic one fails even when it's well written.

## 5.5 Money while you leave: paycheck, loans and insurance

This is the lesson people are most afraid of, so let's make it numbers.

First, you don't have to quit to start. Most of the transitions I can document happened alongside a paycheck. Going to four days buys you one continuous day, which is worth more than five scattered evenings. Telepractice can free up time, but it's still clinical work.

Second, loans. Public Service Loan Forgiveness needs 120 qualifying payments, and they don't have to be in a row. Leaving pauses the count. It doesn't erase it. And it's the employer that qualifies, not your job title. One warning: you have to be in qualifying employment at your 120th payment and when you apply. Don't resign at 118.

Third, health insurance. COBRA runs about 18 months at up to 102 percent of the full premium. The marketplace is often cheaper if your income actually drops.

Fourth, your C's. If certification lapses past twelve months, getting it back needs a recent Praxis score. Decide on purpose.

Loan rules have been changing, so check yours at the official sources linked below.

# Module 6: Leap

## 6.1 How career changers get screened, and the six questions (batch 1)

Welcome to Module 6. Interviews, and then the offer.

Career changers get screened out for three reasons, and none of them is that you can't do the job.

One, they doubt you'll stay. Two, they can't map your clinical language onto their work. Three, they see no proof you've invested in the switch.

You've already built the answers. Your sentence from Module 1 answers the first. Your translated bullets answer the second. The thing you made in Module 5 answers the third.

Every good answer has the same shape. Where you're going, how you prepared, and one accomplishment with a number in it.

About burnout. It's a true reason and a bad answer. It confirms the doubt they already have.

## 6.2 Explaining why you're changing careers

"So why are you leaving clinical work?" You will be asked this in every interview. This lesson builds your answer once.

It's called a bridge statement, and it has three parts.

Pull: where you're going and why this work. This is the sentence from Module 1.

Preparation: what you've done to get ready. The conversations you had, the thing you made.

Proof: one accomplishment with a number in it.

Notice what isn't in there. Nothing about what you're escaping. The same fact can almost always be said either way. "I was drowning in paperwork" and "I found I liked building the system more than working inside it" can be the same truth.

Keep it to about forty-five seconds. Longer than that and you start justifying.

Then say it out loud five times. To a partner, a friend, or your phone. The first time will sound rehearsed. By the fifth it sounds like you.

## 6.6 What a real job search takes

I want to give you one set of numbers so that silence doesn't break you.

One transition I can document took 113 tailored applications, 7 interviews, and 1 offer, over eleven months.

Postings now average around 242 applicants. That's why applying on day one beats sending a perfect application on day nine.

So track four numbers. Applications sent. Phone screens. Interviews. Offers.

Once you have twenty logged, the ratios tell you which stage is broken. Applications and no screens: your résumé is still reading clinical. Screens and no interviews: your answer to "why are you leaving" is about what you're escaping. Interviews and no offers: usually proof, and one thing you made fixes more of that than another certificate.

Without the numbers, every rejection feels like a judgment on you. With them, it's a rate, and you can work on a rate.

Rejection at this stage is a volume statistic. The math only works if you keep feeding it.
