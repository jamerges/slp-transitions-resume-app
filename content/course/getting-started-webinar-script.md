# Getting Started, for SLPs: webinar script

About twenty-five minutes at a natural pace. Talking head over slides,
one take, evening is fine. Slide cues are in brackets; say the words, not
the cues. Every number here is from research-facts.md; where a figure
depends on the viewer's path, the script sends them to their quiz result
rather than guessing.

Biography used: James is a former SLP, now a copywriter and content
strategist at a mental-health-tech company, and hosts the Xceptional Leaders
podcast. Nothing else about him is claimed.

---

[Slide: title. "Getting started, for SLPs." Your name. No tagline.]

Hi. I'm James. I was a speech-language pathologist, and now I write for a
mental-health-tech company. I'm recording this in the evening, so if it's a
Saturday morning where you are, ignore the lamp.

You're here for one of three reasons. You want out and haven't said it to
anyone. You've said it and don't know what else you could do. Or you know
what you want and you're applying and nothing is coming back. This is
twenty-five minutes for all three, and at the end you'll have one number,
one verdict and one thing to do this week. Not a plan for the year. One
thing.

[Slide: "What this is not."]

Two things I won't do. I won't tell you the profession is broken and you
should run. Plenty of SLPs are happy, and some of you will do the exercises
in here and decide to stay, and that's a good outcome. And I won't give you
a list of twenty jobs and wish you luck. Lists are why you're still
scrolling at eleven at night.

[Slide: the five stages, as five stops on a line.]

Here's the first thing I noticed reading a few hundred SLPs talking about
this online, in the groups and the forums and the emails people send me.
Everyone is at one of five stages, and almost everyone thinks they're the
only one there.

Stage one, private doubt. You haven't told anyone. You read exit stories in
bed and close the tab.

Stage two, guilt. The degree, the loans, the people you'd leave, the family
who introduced you as "the speech therapist" for six years.

Stage three, permission. You'd go if someone told you it's allowed.

Stage four, panic. You want out and you have no idea what else you could
do. You've searched "jobs for SLPs" and got a list of school districts.

Stage five, action. You know what you want. You're applying, and the
applications go into a hole.

Pick yours. It changes what you should do next, and I'll come back to it.

[Slide: "It's the workplace, the work, or the season."]

Second thing. Before anything else, you need a verdict on one question,
because three different problems feel identical from inside a bad Tuesday.

Sometimes it's the workplace. The profession is fine and this building
isn't. A specific administrator, a specific productivity number, a
specific caseload count. That's a job problem, and it has a faster fix than
leaving the field.

Sometimes it's the work. The conditions could be perfect and you'd still
feel it. You're more interested in the data, or the training, or the
device, or the system around the therapy than in the therapy. That's a fit
problem, and it doesn't go away when you change settings. If you've already
moved from schools to a clinic and the feeling followed you, that's your
answer.

Sometimes it's the season. A new baby, a diagnosis, grief, a move. Any job
would feel impossible right now. This one deserves real caution, because
burnt-out brains make big decisions badly, and a decision made in a bad
season tends to get unmade in a better one.

There's a free lesson in the course that walks you through six questions and
gives you the verdict. Do it before you do anything I say after this,
because the rest only helps if it's the work.

[Slide: "Your starting line," with three blanks: an hour, a floor, a date.]

Third thing, and this is where most people skip ahead. You need three
numbers before you look at a single job posting.

The first is what an hour of your work actually pays. Not your salary. Your
salary divided by the hours you really work, including the evenings and the
Sunday notes. When SLPs do this arithmetic honestly the number is usually
lower than they'd say out loud, and it changes how a "pay cut" reads.

The second is the number your next job has to clear. Your real monthly
expenses, times twelve, grossed up for tax. That's your floor. Below it you
decline. Above it, everything else is negotiable. If you don't know this
number, every posting is either terrifying or too good to be true, and you
can't tell which.

The third is a date. Not "someday." A month and a year. It's allowed to
move. It's not allowed to be blank.

The free lesson does the arithmetic for you and keeps the numbers. Write
them down somewhere anyway.

[Slide: "The mistake almost everyone makes first."]

Now here's the pattern I'd bet money on. The most common first move is to
open the résumé and try to make it sound less clinical. Swap caseload for
portfolio, parents for stakeholders, IEP meeting for cross-functional
alignment. And then apply to fifty things and get two screens.

Here's why it fails. A résumé is a flyer for one company, and you haven't
named the company. You don't know which of twenty paths you're aiming at,
what it pays, what it needs, or who inside is going to read the thing. So
the flyer says "orchestrated end-to-end client workflows for premium
accounts" and the person reading it can't picture a single day of your
life. The translated résumé is the last step, not the first. Everything
before it is what makes it work.

[Slide: "Twenty paths, one column that matters."]

So, the paths. There are twenty documented ones in our research file, with
salary ranges from real sources rather than from a coach's memory. I'm not
going to read twenty. I'll tell you how to read the list.

Sort by timeline, not by pay. Some of these you can move into in months
because the licence is the qualification: clinical liaison, utilization
review, clinical educator at a device or speech-tech company, customer
success at a teletherapy platform. Some take a year or more of building
something first: instructional design, data, product. And a couple have
guardrails nobody mentions on the sales pages: Epic certification needs an
employer to sponsor you, so "get Epic certified" is not a plan; medical
science liaison is doctorate-gated; UX research is saturated.

One more thing. Some of these pay less than you make now, and I'll say so.
Clinical research coordinator sits below where most SLPs already are. It
can be a bridge with a date on it. It's a bad destination.

The quiz on the site ranks these for you in two minutes, with the range
and the timeline for your top three. Do that instead of the reading the
whole list.

[Slide: "The three doubts."]

Once you know the path, here's what you're actually up against. Across the
documented transitions, a strong clinician gets passed over for three
reasons, and none of them is that you can't do the job.

Retention doubt. Will this person go back to clinical work the moment it
gets hard? Six years of training and a licence look, from outside, like a
strong pull backwards.

Jargon they can't map. Your experience is described in words that mean
nothing to a hiring manager outside healthcare, so they can't tell whether
you've done the job or not.

No proof of investment. Nothing on the page shows you've spent an hour of
your own time on this field. Anyone can say they're interested.

Every part of what comes next answers one of those three. Your reasons
answer the first. Translation answers the second. One thing you built
answers the third.

[Slide: "The people who already made the move."]

Now the part SLPs find hardest, and the part that decides more of these
than anything else. The people.

Your network is almost entirely SLPs. That's not a failing. It's what
twelve years in one profession does. It means nobody you'd call today can
refer you, and referrals decide most of the documented transitions. One
person in our file sent over five hundred applications without one. She got
there, fully remote, more than she made clinically. It took her six months
of that.

Start closer than you think. Your grad cohort: small programs, findable,
and five years out some of them are in health-tech and device companies.
The reps who walk into your building: the AAC vendor, the EMR trainer who
ran your go-live. They hold the jobs on your list, they already know you,
and taking your call is their job. The CF you supervised who never came
back. And the products already on your cart: the EMR, the teletherapy
platform, the assessment publisher. Every one of those companies hires
people who can explain the clinical side, and you're a reference customer.

Then one message. Fifteen minutes about how they got the first one. No job
ask. Make it easy to say yes: one direct question, two time windows. Expect
some silence; it's a full inbox, not a verdict. Three messages is usually
two conversations.

[Slide: "The résumé, last."]

Now, and only now, the résumé.

Every bullet answers three questions. How big was it. What did you do. What
changed. "Managed a caseload of 62 with a plan, a timeline and a quarterly
review for each" is a sentence a hiring manager can picture. "Orchestrated
client workflows" is not.

Keep your title. Speech-Language Pathologist stays. Renaming it is the one
screening flag nobody recovers from. The translation goes in a function
line underneath.

Numbers, and a test for them: if you couldn't talk for ten minutes about
the day behind that number without inventing anything, take it off.

And the colleague test. If any SLP down the hall could have written the
same line about their own year, it's a job description, not evidence, and
it goes.

[Slide: "One thing you built."]

Last piece. Proof. A certificate says you paid. A thing you made says you
can do the job. For most paths that's one artifact from work you've already
done: the training you ran for nurses rebuilt as a ten-minute module, your
own progress data rebuilt in a spreadsheet with one chart, a one-page
onboarding plan for a product you know. Two ninety-minute sessions. It goes
on LinkedIn, in the résumé, and into every interview as a story.

[Slide: "What to do this week," by stage.]

So, back to your stage. One thing each.

Private doubt: do the verdict lesson. Tell nobody yet. That's allowed.

Guilt: do the money page. The number is usually kinder than the guilt.

Permission: you have it. Send one message to one person who left, this
week, with the template on the site.

Panic: take the quiz and read only your top three. Not the whole list.

Action: stop applying for two weeks. Get three conversations and one
artifact, then apply to five postings under 48 hours old, tailored, with a
person inside each one.

I read every reply. If something in here was wrong for your situation, tell

You're allowed to want out. And you're allowed to find out it's the
building, not the work, and stay. Either way, do the twenty minutes.

Thanks for watching.
