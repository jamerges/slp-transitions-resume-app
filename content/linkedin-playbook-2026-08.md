3. **Day-series from the interviews.** "5 SLPs, 5 days, 5 different exits."
   One guest per day, each with the concrete numbers (starting salary band,

   guest their day.
4. **Funnel-numbers post.** The $200-article-to-agency story, or any guest

   (liaison $84–135k / 1–3 months; UR $80–88k; CS $75–120k) reformatted
   1200×1500. Three posts, not one.
6. **Contrarian data read.** Her highest-comment analytical post questioned
   LinkedIn's "PT = #3 fastest-growing job" list (17 comments). Ours: what
   the BLS "growing profession" number hides about why SLPs still leave —
   caseloads, productivity requirements, the license ceiling.
7. **The guilt post.** Her "who will be left to treat?" pulled 13 comments.
   We have the researched version: should-you-quit-slp (blog 3397).
   Repurpose as a text post ending on the question, link in comments.
8. **Bad-advice correction.** "The most common bad advice SLPs get about
   clinical informatics: 'just get Epic certified.' You can't — Epic
   certification is employer-sponsored. Here's the door that's actually open."
   Factual-guardrail content nobody else in the niche runs.
9. **Translation pairs.** "Caseload of 60 → portfolio of 60 concurrent
   clients." The retired homepage strip is a carousel; pairs live in
   content/blog/03 + build_home.py TRANSLATIONS.
10. **AOTA-equivalent visibility.** When guests speak (Levy/Seaver, ASHA
    Centennial), post as their interviewer, tag them.

Rules carried over: end on a question; tag real people; no hashtag walls

## Founder-led content layer (Kristen Lowe archetype framework, added 2026-08-11)

James's archetype: **Problem founder** (built the thing he needed while
leaving), with a secondary Insight pillar (research guardrails). Goal:
readers feel recognized, not impressed. Success signal per the framework:
people start telling THEIR story in comments/inbox — which is also our VoC
and interview pipeline. Converges with the Hannah data: her top-performing
format (time-anchored personal reflection) IS the origin-story pillar.

Mistakes to guard against, mapped: (1) slipping into CEO/marketer persona —
no posts about funnels, conversion, "the market"; James is an SLP who left,
not a SaaS operator, in this feed's frame. (2) Overgeneralizing to
"clinicians" — stay SLP-specific, it's the moat. (3) The origin story must
be scene-level, not summary-level.

### Product-design story drafts (ready to post)

**P1 — The step where everyone quit**
> People kept abandoning my product at the exact same step: the one where I
> asked for their resume.
>
> It made no sense until I pictured when SLPs actually use a career quiz.
> It's 9:30 on a weeknight. You're on your phone. Your resume is on a
> laptop you haven't opened since the last job hunt.
>
> I was asking for a document nobody has in their hand at the moment they
> need the tool.
>
> So I flipped the order. Now the report comes first and the resume comes
> after — with an email link so you can finish from the laptop, whenever
> you're actually near it.
>
> The product wasn't broken. The moment was wrong.
>
> If you've ever quit a career tool halfway through — what stopped you?

**P2 — The banned-words list**
> There's a list of words my resume tool is not allowed to use.
>
> "Passionate" is on it. So is "seamless." So is "leverage."
>
> Recruiters read hundreds of AI-written resumes a week now, and those words
> are the tell. The whole point of translating clinical experience is that
> it sounds like a person who did the work — because you did.
>
> So the tool writes around them. Every draft, checked against the list.
>
> What's a resume word you never want to read again?

**P3 — The tool that refuses to flatter you**
> My resume tool isn't allowed to invent numbers about you.
>
> If you never told it your caseload size, it won't guess. It writes
> [bracketed placeholder] and hands the blank back to you.
>
> Because a fabricated "32% improvement in outcomes" survives exactly until
> an interviewer asks about it.
>
> Slower? A little. But you walk into the interview able to defend every
> line, and that's the entire job of a resume.

**P4 — The locked door (Insight pillar)**
> The most common advice SLPs get about clinical informatics is a locked
> door: "just get Epic certified."
>
> You can't. Epic certification is employer-sponsored — you get it by
> already working for a hospital that runs Epic, not the other way around.
>
> The door that actually opens: sponsor-track analyst roles, where the
> hospital certifies you after hiring. Different search terms, different
> resume, same destination.
>
> (This is why I put factual guardrails in everything I build — the niche
> runs on recycled advice nobody re-checks.)

### Origin-story scaffold (needs James's scenes)

Format: time-anchored opening → one specific scene → the turn → what it
became. Answer these four (adapted from the framework's Problem-founder
prompts) and the posts write themselves:
1. The single worst day you had as a clinician — the scene, not the theme.
   Where were you sitting? What was the time? What was the task?
2. The tipping point: the moment you decided to actually leave rather than
   cope. What triggered it that day, specifically?
3. When you realized it wasn't just you — first conversation/post/DM where
   another SLP described your exact situation.
4. Why you had to build the fix for others once you were out.
