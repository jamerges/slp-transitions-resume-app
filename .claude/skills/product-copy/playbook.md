# Product Copy + UX Playbook
## A prompt-ready operating system for landing pages, apps, workbooks, onboarding, and marketing copy

**Built for:** SLP Transitions, Same Page, and future product projects  
**Primary sources synthesized:** Harry Dry / Marketing Examples, Demand Curve, VeryGoodCopy / Eddie Shleyner  
**Purpose:** Give Claude, ChatGPT, or another AI a durable set of principles for improving product positioning, UX writing, landing pages, conversion copy, workbooks, and in-product messaging.

> This is a synthesis, not a transcript. The principles below are paraphrased and reorganized into a practical system.

---

# 1. The governing idea

Great product copy reduces the amount of thinking a user has to do.

A strong page or interface helps someone understand, in roughly this order:

1. **What is this?**
2. **Is it for someone like me?**
3. **Why should I care?**
4. **What will I get?**
5. **Why should I believe you?**
6. **What could go wrong or hold me back?**
7. **What do I do next?**
8. **What happens after I click?**

The job is not to sound “market-y.” The job is to make the value easy to see, the next step easy to take, and the claims easy to trust.

A useful conversion model, adapted from Demand Curve:

**Conversion likelihood rises when Desire + Confidence increase, and Effort + Confusion decrease.**

Use that as the master diagnostic for both UX and marketing copy.

---

# 2. The non-negotiable hierarchy

When principles conflict, prioritize them in this order:

1. **Clarity**
2. **Credibility**
3. **Relevance**
4. **Specificity**
5. **Ease**
6. **Momentum**
7. **Personality**
8. **Cleverness**

Clever copy that delays comprehension is usually bad copy.

Personality is valuable only after the reader knows what is happening.

Short is not automatically better. Long is not automatically better. The goal is the shortest version that answers the reader’s important questions and removes enough doubt to let them act.

---

# 3. Start with the customer, not the sentence

Before rewriting, collect the raw material.

## Ask:

- What is the user trying to accomplish?
- What are they doing instead today?
- What is frustrating about the current way?
- What are they afraid will happen?
- What have they already tried?
- What words do they use to describe the problem?
- What does success look like in concrete terms?
- What objections stop them from buying or continuing?
- What makes this product meaningfully different?
- What does the product *actually* do better than alternatives?
- Where are users most skeptical?
- What proof exists?
- What claims should **not** be made?
- Who is the product not for?

Harry Dry and Eddie Shleyner both repeatedly emphasize using the customer’s own language. Strong copy is usually discovered in interviews, support messages, reviews, sales calls, Reddit threads, survey responses, and real user behavior before it is “written.”

## Research rule

Do not invent a pain point because it sounds persuasive.

Do not invent a benefit because it sounds aspirational.

Do not invent proof.

If evidence is unavailable, phrase the claim modestly or ask for evidence.

---

# 4. Write for one reader

A page may get 10,000 visits. It is still read by one person at a time.

Prefer:

- “you”
- “your”
- “here’s what happens”
- “you’ll see”
- “if you’re…”

Avoid institutional language like:

- “users are able to”
- “individuals can”
- “our solution enables”
- “our innovative platform empowers”

Write like an intelligent person explaining the product to another intelligent person across a table.

The copy should sound spoken, not delivered from a stage.

---

# 5. Value before features

A feature describes the mechanism.

A benefit describes what the user can now do, understand, avoid, save, feel, or achieve.

A strong product section often follows:

**Benefit → mechanism → proof → objection handling → action**

Example:

Weak:
> AI resume analysis

Better:
> See exactly where your clinical experience matches the job, where it falls short, and how to rewrite it in language hiring managers recognize.

Then explain the mechanism:
> Upload your resume and the job description. The tool compares them requirement by requirement.

This principle appears repeatedly in Harry Dry’s work and Demand Curve’s landing-page guidance.

## Feature-to-value ladder

For every feature, ask:

1. What does it do?
2. So what?
3. What does that let the user do?
4. Why does that matter?
5. What frustration disappears?
6. What positive outcome becomes possible?

Do not stop at the first answer.

---

# 6. Specific beats impressive

Vague claims are easy to write and hard to believe.

Weak:
- Save time
- Work smarter
- Unlock your potential
- Get better insights
- Transform your career
- Supercharge collaboration
- Powerful analytics
- Personalized guidance

Stronger:
- Compare your resume against the job’s actual requirements.
- See which requirements you already cover, where you have partial evidence, and what is still missing.
- Get three rewritten bullets before you pay.
- Analyze a WhatsApp export in your browser without uploading the conversation.
- See how your communication patterns changed month by month.

Specificity creates credibility because the reader can picture what happens.

## Specificity can come from:

- numbers
- time
- audience
- mechanism
- scope
- output
- before/after contrast
- constraints
- price
- workflow
- named use cases
- concrete examples

But never add fake precision.

---

# 7. Find the product’s “magic”

Demand Curve calls for identifying what is unusually compelling about the product. Harry Dry often frames this as the hook.

Ask:

**What is the most surprising, valuable, or relieving thing that becomes true because this product exists?**

Potential categories:

- dramatically easier
- dramatically faster
- unusually private
- unusually affordable
- removes a hated step
- reveals something previously invisible
- combines tasks that required multiple tools
- serves a neglected audience unusually well
- converts expertise into an output the customer could not easily produce alone
- makes a scary process feel navigable
- creates a “how is that possible?” moment

The hook should usually be understandable without explanation.

---

# 8. The hero section

The hero has a hard job. It should quickly communicate:

1. **Value**
2. **Mechanism**
3. **Audience or context, when useful**
4. **Proof or reassurance**
5. **Next step**

A reliable pattern:

### Headline
State the valuable outcome, distinctive mechanism, or strongest positioning idea.

### Subheadline
Explain how the product produces that value. Add audience/context if needed.

### Visual
Show the product, output, or transformation. Avoid decorative illustrations when a real product screenshot, sample output, demo, or before/after can do the job.

### Trust cue
Add proof, privacy, price, reversibility, or friction-reducing information near the CTA.

### CTA
Continue the promise instead of merely describing the click.

---

# 9. Headline rules

A headline should usually do at least one of these:

- communicate the main benefit
- identify the audience
- make a useful promise
- express a compelling contrast
- address a major objection
- communicate a new or surprising mechanism
- create curiosity that the next line immediately resolves

Harry Dry’s core test is essentially: can someone glance at the headline and know why they should care?

VeryGoodCopy emphasizes useful, specific, reader-centered headlines rather than writer-centered cleverness.

## Good headline characteristics

- concrete
- conversational
- easy to repeat
- easy to understand out of context
- aligned with the product’s actual value
- strong enough to carry the page without visual tricks

## Avoid

- empty category language
- abstract mission statements
- generic inspiration
- inflated adjectives
- claims that require five seconds of interpretation
- rhymes or wordplay that obscure the offer

## Headline stress test

Ask:

- Could a competitor paste this onto their site unchanged?
- Does this describe a real benefit?
- Does the reader know what kind of product this is?
- Is there a sharper, more concrete version?
- Is there an objection worth answering here?
- Does it sound like a human would say it?

If almost any competitor could use it, keep working.

---

# 10. Subtitle rules

The subtitle should make the headline more believable and more concrete.

Useful ingredients:

- what the product is
- how it works
- what input the user provides
- what output they get
- how long it takes
- what makes the mechanism different

A subtitle is not a second slogan.

Think:

**Headline = why I care**  
**Subtitle = how this actually works**

---

# 11. Calls to action: sell the next moment

Harry Dry recommends thinking in terms of a **call to value**, not merely a call to action.

Weak:
- Submit
- Continue
- Learn more
- Get started
- Sign up
- Request

Stronger:
- See my matches
- Translate my resume
- Analyze my chat
- Try a sample
- Build my plan
- Show me the gaps
- Preview my results

The strongest CTA often completes the sentence:

> I want to ______.

## CTA rules

- Describe the next meaningful value.
- Match the commitment to the visitor’s level of intent.
- For cold visitors, consider a lower-friction action.
- Repeat the CTA after important persuasion sections.
- Keep the primary action visually dominant.
- Use secondary CTAs sparingly.
- Tell users what happens next when uncertainty might stop them.

Microcopy near the CTA can remove hesitation:

- No account required.
- Takes about 3 minutes.
- Nothing is uploaded.
- Preview free before paying.
- Cancel anytime.
- No credit card required.

Use only true statements.

---

# 12. Momentum: the “slippery slide”

Harry Dry, drawing from direct-response tradition, emphasizes that each line should make the next line easier to read.

This is partly copy and partly layout.

## Create momentum with:

- short first lines
- concrete subheads
- unanswered but immediately resolved questions
- contrast
- mini-stories
- specific claims
- rhythm
- visual hierarchy
- progressive disclosure
- meaningful transitions

Avoid forcing readers through dense explanation before earning their interest.

A page should feel like a sequence of useful answers, not a brochure.

---

# 13. Scannability is persuasion

People skim even when they care.

Design the copy for skimming.

## Use:

- short paragraphs
- descriptive subheads
- bullets
- bolding only for genuinely useful emphasis
- compact sections
- strong visual grouping
- whitespace
- clear labels
- consistent hierarchy
- repeated action points

A reader who only scans headings should still understand:

- what this is
- why it matters
- how it works
- why to trust it
- what to do next

Do not hide the argument inside body copy.

---

# 14. Short sentences. Strong verbs.

Harry Dry argues for more periods and fewer commas. The broader principle: reduce sentence load.

Prefer:

> Upload your resume. Add the job description. See where you match.

Over:

> By uploading your resume and adding a job description, our platform is able to analyze your experience and provide a personalized overview of how well your background aligns with the position.

Prefer active verbs:

- compare
- see
- build
- rewrite
- show
- find
- choose
- export
- check
- map
- analyze

Avoid noun-heavy phrases:

- utilization of
- implementation of
- facilitation of
- optimization of

---

# 15. Cut adjectives before facts

Adjectives are often placeholders for proof.

Weak:
- powerful
- seamless
- intuitive
- robust
- revolutionary
- cutting-edge
- comprehensive
- world-class
- personalized
- actionable

Ask: **What observable fact is this adjective trying to imply?**

“Comprehensive” may become:
> Covers your resume, cover letter, LinkedIn profile, interview bridges, and 90-day transition plan.

“Private” may become:
> Your chat is processed in the browser. The conversation is not uploaded or stored.

Facts usually sell better than adjectives.

---

# 16. Avoid “landing-page language”

Harry Dry specifically warns against generic promotional verbs such as “unlock,” “unleash,” “empower,” “elevate,” and “supercharge.”

Also be skeptical of:

- transform
- revolutionize
- harness
- optimize
- maximize
- next-level
- game-changing
- seamless
- holistic
- journey
- solution

These words are not forbidden. They simply carry almost no information by themselves.

Replace them with the actual action or outcome whenever possible.

---

# 17. Write how people talk

Conversational does **not** mean goofy.

It means:

- contractions
- ordinary vocabulary
- direct address
- natural sentence fragments when useful
- occasional conjunctions at the start of sentences
- spoken rhythm
- concrete nouns and verbs
- no unnecessary formality

Read the copy aloud.

If it sounds like a press release, rewrite it.

If a normal person would feel embarrassed saying it to a customer face-to-face, rewrite it.

---

# 18. Personality should sharpen, not obscure

A distinctive voice is useful because faceless product language is forgettable.

But personality should serve comprehension.

Good personality can:

- make an uncomfortable topic easier
- signal empathy
- create warmth
- make the product memorable
- communicate confidence
- establish a worldview

Bad personality:

- jokes during high-anxiety actions
- quirky labels that make navigation harder
- cute error messages that do not explain the problem
- trying to sound edgy when the user needs reassurance
- putting the writer’s wit above the user’s goal

---

# 19. Tension creates interest

Harry Dry recommends finding the tension.

Many good positioning ideas contain a conflict:

- old way vs. new way
- what professionals were trained to do vs. what the system forces them to do
- what the user believes vs. what the data reveals
- expertise vs. how hiring software interprets it
- intimacy vs. how little we notice about our language
- convenience vs. privacy
- ambition vs. uncertainty
- “I know I have transferable skills” vs. “I can’t explain them to a recruiter”

Tension gives the page narrative energy.

Do not manufacture drama. Find the real contradiction already present in the user’s life.

---

# 20. Contrast is one of the best persuasion tools

A product becomes easier to understand when compared against a familiar alternative.

Useful contrasts:

- Before / after
- Old way / new way
- Generic tool / purpose-built tool
- Guessing / knowing
- Feature / outcome
- Manual / automatic
- Uploaded / stays in browser
- Clinical wording / hiring-manager wording
- Raw metrics / understandable story

Contrast creates meaning faster than additional description.

---

# 21. Objections belong in the page, not outside it

Do not hope users forget their objections.

Name them and answer them.

Common categories:

- price
- time
- difficulty
- privacy
- trust
- accuracy
- “will this work for someone like me?”
- “is this just AI?”
- “what if I don’t know what I want yet?”
- “what happens to my data?”
- “what if the result is wrong?”
- “do I need another account?”
- “what do I actually receive?”

Demand Curve recommends pairing value propositions with objection handling throughout the feature sections, then catching remaining questions in the FAQ.

## Objection-handling structure

1. Name the concern plainly.
2. Give the shortest truthful answer.
3. Explain the mechanism or policy.
4. Add proof when possible.
5. Tell the user what control they retain.

---

# 22. Proof: show, don’t merely reassure

Social proof is not decoration. It answers:

**“Why should I believe this will work for me?”**

Useful proof:

- quantified outcomes
- testimonials
- customer quotes
- before/after examples
- screenshots
- recognizable customer logos
- number of users
- concrete case studies
- samples
- demonstrations
- transparent methodology
- citations
- credible limitations

Demand Curve distinguishes quick credibility proof near the top from richer proof farther down.

## Strong testimonial characteristics

The best testimonial says:

- who the person is
- what problem they had
- what changed
- what concrete value they received
- what objection was overcome

Weak:
> Amazing product!

Stronger:
> I’d been applying to customer success roles for months. Seeing exactly which clinical examples mapped to the job description helped me rewrite my bullets around implementation, training, and stakeholder communication.

Never fabricate or “improve” testimonials beyond light editing that preserves meaning.

---

# 23. Product visuals should explain value

Demand Curve and Harry Dry both favor showing the product itself rather than generic decoration.

For software, prioritize:

1. Product in action
2. Sample output
3. Before/after
4. Annotated screenshot
5. Simple animation showing the workflow
6. Relevant diagram

Avoid:

- abstract blobs
- generic people at laptops
- stock photos unrelated to use
- screenshots so tiny no one can understand them
- decorative dashboards that do not prove anything

The visual should answer a question or make the promise feel real.

---

# 24. Design should reduce interpretation

Do not make the interface “unique” at the expense of familiarity.

Familiar patterns are often useful because users already know how they work.

Prefer:

- normal top navigation
- recognizable buttons
- clear form fields
- standard progress patterns
- obvious accordions
- visible back navigation
- consistent spacing
- predictable hierarchy

Novelty belongs in the product value, not in forcing users to relearn a button.

---

# 25. One screen, one dominant job

Every screen or section should have a primary purpose.

Examples:

- Explain the value
- Upload the input
- Choose the goal
- Review the result
- Resolve a concern
- Upgrade
- Save/export
- Continue

If five elements compete equally, the interface has no priority.

Ask:

**What is the one action or idea this screen must make easiest?**

Make that visually dominant.

---

# 26. Progressive disclosure

Do not explain everything at once.

Show:

- essential information first
- details when requested
- methodology after the user understands the result
- caveats near claims where they matter
- deep technical information behind “How this works” when appropriate

This is especially important for technically grounded or research-informed products.

Users should be able to understand the top-line result without reading a paper, while still being able to inspect the reasoning.

---

# 27. Microcopy is product design

VeryGoodCopy treats microcopy as a tool for helping people start and finish actions.

Microcopy should do at least one of these:

- clarify
- anticipate a question
- prevent an error
- explain why something is needed
- reduce anxiety
- set expectations
- resolve an objection
- help recover from failure

## Useful places for microcopy

- form fields
- upload areas
- paywalls
- pricing
- empty states
- permissions
- privacy choices
- loading states
- error messages
- destructive actions
- export/download actions
- AI-generated outputs
- uncertain or probabilistic results

## Error-message formula

Instead of:
> Something went wrong.

Use:
> We couldn’t read that file. Try a WhatsApp `.txt` or `.zip` export without media.

When possible include:

1. what happened
2. why it may have happened
3. what to do next

---

# 28. Trust copy belongs at the moment of anxiety

Do not put all reassurance in a distant FAQ.

If someone is about to upload a private chat, privacy copy belongs beside the upload.

If someone is about to pay, refund or one-time-pricing copy belongs beside the price.

If an AI result is probabilistic, the limitation belongs beside the result.

Good UX answers doubt where doubt occurs.

---

# 29. Confidence without overclaiming

VeryGoodCopy recommends confident copy, but confidence does not mean certainty you cannot support.

Confident:
> We compare your resume against the job’s requirements and show where your evidence is strong, partial, or missing.

Overclaim:
> We guarantee you’ll land the job.

Confident:
> This shows patterns in how you text.

Overclaim:
> Your texts reveal whether your relationship will last.

The strongest trustworthy copy knows exactly how far it can go.

---

# 30. Transparency can increase persuasion

A limitation can increase credibility.

Examples:

- “This is a mirror, not a verdict.”
- “We rewrite the content, not your existing resume design.”
- “Text patterns can show shifts in communication. They cannot tell you why the shift happened.”
- “Without timestamps, you’ll still get the core analysis, but the timeline uses message count instead of calendar months.”

Transparency reduces the suspicion that the product is hiding something.

---

# 31. Price framing

Price should not arrive as an unexplained number.

Make the comparison fair and useful.

Possible anchors:

- alternative cost
- time saved
- number of deliverables
- cost of separate services
- cost of continuing the current problem
- one-time vs recurring
- free preview vs paid depth

Avoid manipulative fake discounts and arbitrary crossed-out prices.

Basecamp-style price framing works because it establishes a relevant comparison before the price.

---

# 32. Landing-page architecture

A reliable default:

## 1. Hero
- value-rich headline
- mechanism-rich subtitle
- product visual
- primary CTA
- friction-reducing microcopy

## 2. Fast proof
- user count, result, trusted logos, testimonial, privacy proof, or credibility cue

## 3. Problem / tension
- show the reader you understand the current situation
- name the cost of the old way without melodrama

## 4. Core benefits
For each:
- outcome
- explanation
- product visual
- objection handling
- relevant CTA when useful

## 5. How it works
Prefer 3–4 concrete steps.

## 6. Rich social proof
- testimonials
- case examples
- result screenshots
- sample outputs

## 7. Price / offer
- what is included
- what is not
- pricing
- guarantee/refund if applicable
- CTA

## 8. FAQ
Catch:
- objections
- edge cases
- repeated support questions
- audience fit
- privacy
- pricing
- limitations

## 9. Final CTA
Restate the best reason to act.

## 10. Focused footer
On conversion-specific landing pages, remove nonessential exits.

This is a template, not a law. Remove sections that do not earn their space.

---

# 33. Match the landing page to the traffic

Demand Curve strongly recommends message matching.

If someone arrives from:

- an ad about burnout → continue the burnout-to-career-change story
- a search for “SLP non-clinical jobs” → speak directly to that problem
- a quiz result → continue from the result
- a post about communication synchrony → explain that mechanism
- a privacy-focused referral → foreground local browser processing

Do not make every visitor restart from a generic homepage.

The ad, email, post, search query, and landing page should feel like one continuous conversation.

---

# 34. Forms: remove unnecessary labor

Every field creates friction.

Ask:

- Do we truly need this field now?
- Could we infer it?
- Could we ask later?
- Could we provide a sample?
- Can we explain why we need it?
- Can the user continue without creating an account?
- Can we preserve progress?

For multi-step flows, show progress if it reduces uncertainty.

Avoid asking for personal information before the user understands the value exchange.

---

# 35. Workbooks: treat instruction copy like UX copy

A workbook is an interface on paper or PDF.

Every exercise should answer:

1. What am I doing?
2. Why am I doing it?
3. How long should it take?
4. What does “good enough” look like?
5. What should I do if I get stuck?
6. What do I do with the answer next?

## Better workbook structure

### Exercise title
Outcome-oriented, not academic.

### Why this matters
1–3 sentences.

### Instruction
One action at a time.

### Example
Show one realistic answer.

### Prompt
Ask a concrete question.

### Space / structure
Give the right amount of room for the expected answer.

### Next step
Explain where this input goes later.

Avoid large blank boxes after vague prompts such as:
> Reflect on your values.

Better:
> Circle the five values you would protect even if a new job paid more. Then rank those five. You’ll use your top three to score career options on page 12.

---

# 36. AI-generated product copy needs extra care

When an AI writes for the user, UX copy should clearly communicate:

- what input is being used
- what output is being generated
- what is automatic vs interpretive
- whether content leaves the device
- whether the output may be wrong
- how to edit or regenerate
- whether the user keeps control
- what happens to data afterward

Avoid anthropomorphizing the AI when it creates false confidence.

Prefer:
> Drafted from your resume and the job description.

Over:
> Our AI understands your unique professional journey.

---

# 37. High-trust products: accuracy is part of conversion

For career, mental-health-adjacent, relationship, scientific, financial, or health-related products, conversion cannot be separated from epistemic trust.

Good marketing should make claims more precise, not more absolute.

Use:

- source links
- methodology summaries
- caveats
- confidence levels where appropriate
- examples
- “what this can and cannot tell you”
- user controls

This is particularly important for Same Page.

# 38. Project-specific rules: SLP Transitions

SLP Transitions serves people who may feel burned out, stuck, professionally insecure, or unsure how their clinical experience translates.

The copy should create **agency**, not shame.

## Emphasize

- concrete career outcomes
- skill translation
- real hiring language
- specificity by role
- visible next steps
- practical artifacts
- realistic salary / role information when sourced
- “you already have evidence; we help translate it”
- low-friction experimentation
- examples from SLPs who made similar pivots

## Avoid

- implying clinical work was wasted
- promising an effortless career change
- generic empowerment language
- framing every user as “burned out”
- implying a resume rewrite alone guarantees a job
- overloading users with a huge transition plan before giving them a quick win

## Strong product narrative

**Problem:** Clinical experience is valuable, but hiring systems often do not recognize clinical language.

**Tension:** The user may be qualified for adjacent work yet look unqualified on paper.

**Mechanism:** Translate real clinical work into the requirements and vocabulary of a specific role.

**Proof:** Requirement-by-requirement mapping, rewritten examples, successful-transition stories, sample outputs.

**Outcome:** The user knows where they fit, what to rewrite, what gaps to close, and what to do next.

## Current copy strength

“Your SLP resume, translated into a career you actually want” is directionally strong because it combines audience context, mechanism, and desired outcome.

Continue to make the rest of the experience as concrete as that promise.

# 39. Project-specific rules: Same Page

Same Page is not primarily selling analytics. It is selling **a new way to notice a relationship through language**.

The product has unusually high privacy and interpretation sensitivity.

## Emphasize

- curiosity
- recognition
- patterns over verdicts
- “see what changed”
- actual moments from the conversation
- local/browser processing
- transparent methodology
- scientific humility
- user interpretation
- emotionally intelligent language

## Avoid

- diagnosing the relationship
- predicting breakup or compatibility
- turning a descriptive metric into a score of relationship quality
- sensational claims from small correlations
- implying that a text analysis knows intent
- making conflict labels feel clinically authoritative

## Strong product narrative

**Problem:** A relationship changes gradually, but the people inside it rarely see the pattern clearly.

**Tension:** We remember the big fights and romantic moments, not the thousands of tiny language choices in between.

**Mechanism:** Analyze small-word patterns, timing, responsiveness, friction, and repair across the conversation.

**Trust:** Processing stays in the browser; the tool distinguishes descriptive signals from predictions and exposes its assumptions.

**Outcome:** The user sees the rhythm of the conversation from another angle and has better questions to ask, not a verdict to obey.

“Small words. Big story.” is a strong brand line because it creates contrast and curiosity. It should be paired quickly with literal explanation so curiosity never becomes confusion.

# 40. UX copy by state

AI reviewers should inspect every state, not just the polished happy path.

## Empty state
Tell the user:
- what will appear here
- how to create it
- why it is useful

## Loading state
Tell the user:
- what is happening
- whether they can leave
- what will happen next

Do not fake precision such as “73% complete” unless it is real.

## Error state
Tell the user:
- what failed
- whether anything was lost
- what they can try
- how to get help

## Success state
Confirm:
- what happened
- where the result lives
- what the next useful action is

## Paywall
Explain:
- what is already free
- what becomes available
- the exact price
- recurring vs one-time
- whether the user keeps outputs
- refund/cancellation if relevant

## Destructive action
Explain consequences before confirmation.

# 41. The editing pass

Run copy through these passes in order.

## Pass 1: Meaning
Is every claim true?

## Pass 2: Customer
Is this about the reader or about us?

## Pass 3: Specificity
Can a vague phrase become an observable fact?

## Pass 4: Clarity
Could an intelligent outsider understand this?

## Pass 5: Objections
What question appears in the reader’s mind after each claim?

## Pass 6: Proof
What would make this believable?

## Pass 7: Brevity
Can we express the same idea faster?

## Pass 8: Rhythm
Read it aloud. Does it sound natural?

## Pass 9: Hierarchy
If someone only reads headings and buttons, does the experience still make sense?

## Pass 10: Action
Is the next step obvious and proportionate?

# 42. The “$100-per-word” deletion test

Inspired by Harry Dry’s “write with your eraser” principle:

Pretend every word you remove earns you $100.

Remove:

- duplicated ideas
- throat-clearing
- obvious labels
- adjectives that add no proof
- corporate filler
- repeated context
- needless transitions
- generic enthusiasm
- disclaimers that can be expressed once more clearly

But do not remove words that:

- prevent misunderstanding
- establish trust
- explain the mechanism
- answer a real objection
- reduce anxiety
- protect scientific or ethical accuracy

The goal is **information density**, not minimalism.

# 43. The “would a salesperson say this?” test

Harry Dry suggests thinking of the landing page as the sales pitch.

For each section ask:

> If I were talking to this customer in person, would this sentence help them make a decision?

If no, delete or rewrite it.

This catches:
- decorative slogans
- empty mission language
- irrelevant company history
- generic feature lists
- vanity claims
- filler illustrations

## Outsider
Useful for:

- whether the value is understandable

Useful for:

You need both.

# 45. A compact scoring rubric

Score each 1–5.

## Clarity
Can I understand what this is immediately?

## Relevance
Does it clearly speak to a recognizable user and need?

## Specificity
Are claims concrete rather than generic?

## Value
Does it explain the outcome, not merely the features?

## Credibility
Is there enough proof, mechanism, transparency, or evidence?

## Friction
Is the next step easy and low-anxiety?

## Hierarchy
Can I skim and still understand the argument?

## Voice
Does it sound human and distinct without sacrificing clarity?

## Objection handling
Are the important doubts answered near where they occur?

## CTA
Is the next action obvious and framed around value?

### Interpretation
- **45–50:** unusually strong
- **38–44:** solid, refine details
- **30–37:** understandable but leaving conversion on the table
- **under 30:** reposition or restructure before polishing sentences

Do not let the numerical score create false precision. It is a review tool.

# 47. Copy formulas worth keeping

Formulas are starting points, not templates to force onto every page.

## PAS
**Problem → Agitation → Solution**

Good when the pain is already salient.

Use agitation carefully. Do not manipulate vulnerable users.

## PASP
**Problem → Agitation → Solution → Proof**

Often stronger because it makes the solution believable.

## BAB
**Before → After → Bridge**

Useful for transformations.

## AIDA
**Attention → Interest → Desire → Action**

Useful for campaigns and longer sales flows.

## Promise → Picture → Proof → Push
Useful when the outcome is easy to visualize.

## Benefit → Mechanism → Proof → CTA
Excellent default for product pages.

## Objection → Answer → Evidence → Control
Excellent for high-trust UX.

# 48. Story without fluff

Stories work because they make abstract value concrete.

Use stories to show:

- a recognizable person
- a specific starting problem
- a decision
- a mechanism
- a change
- a result

Avoid long founder lore that does not help the reader understand or trust the product.

A founder note can work late on a landing page when it adds:

- motive
- credibility
- humanity
- worldview
- a memorable closing

# 49. Ethical persuasion rules

Never:

- invent urgency
- fabricate scarcity
- fake testimonials
- fake user counts
- imply scientific certainty that does not exist
- hide recurring billing
- make cancellation deliberately difficult
- bury data-use information
- exploit shame
- scare users into upgrading
- disguise ads or upsells as required actions

Good persuasion reduces uncertainty. Dark patterns exploit it.

# 50. Design + copy integration checklist

A strong interface should pass all of these:

- [ ] The primary action is visually obvious.
- [ ] Headings explain the page when skimmed alone.
- [ ] Body text is readable on mobile.
- [ ] Paragraphs are short.
- [ ] Important reassurance appears near risky actions.
- [ ] Screenshots are large enough to understand.
- [ ] Forms ask only for necessary information.
- [ ] Buttons use meaningful labels.
- [ ] Links look like links.
- [ ] Secondary actions do not compete with the primary action.
- [ ] Empty, loading, error, and success states are written.
- [ ] Pricing clearly states recurring vs one-time.
- [ ] Product claims have nearby support.
- [ ] Jargon is defined or removed.
- [ ] The page works if the reader ignores 70% of the body copy.
- [ ] Mobile is treated as a primary layout, not an afterthought.
- [ ] The experience does not require hover to understand essential information.
- [ ] Privacy and data use are stated at the moment users provide sensitive inputs.

# 58. Final operating principle

Do not ask:

> “How can we make this sound better?”

> “What does the user need to understand, believe, feel safe about, and do next?”
